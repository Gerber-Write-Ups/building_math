		\section{Introduction}
			The purpose of this monograph is to present a formalization of set theory and, thereby, mathematics.
			It is known that traditional mathematics can be expressed in terms of mathematical set theory and that statements within set theory can expressed in terms of a first order language and that proofs in set theory can be constructed in the context of a corresponding first order logic.
			We are interested in formalizing mathematics because, once formalized, the correctness of stated theorems becomes a matter of programmatic rule following/deconstruction rather than a matter of using cleverness and intuition to understand natural language statements.
			
			In this document, the formalization of mathematics will involve the development of the first order language and logic system.			
			We will generically refer to the language as the \gls{lfol} and to the logic system as \gls{fol}.
			\gls{lfol} will be composed from explicitly and clearly defined symbols constituting the alphabet of the language and syntax rules which identify \qq{appropriate} ways to combine the symbol to form valid formulas.
			\gls{fol} will include a scheme for constructing proofs.
			A proof will be a sequence of lines where each line includes a statement from \gls{lfol} together with context about how that proof line was derived and on which previous lines that proof depends.
			We derive new lines of a proof from old lines using the deduction rules of the \gls{fol}.
			By examining valid proofs and which statements of \gls{lfol} depend (proof theoretically) on which other statements, we can construct logical theories.
			A logical theory says that if we assume some set $\Gamma$ of axioms then we can derive, using the deduction rules $R$, a larger set of statements $T_R(\Gamma)$.
			To develop set theory we will select certain statements within $\gls{lfol}$ to server as our set theory axiom set $\Gamma_{\text{Set}}$.
			All of mathematics is then the process of determing whether some statement $S$ within \gls{lfol} is in the theory $T(\Gamma_{\text{Set}})$.
			That is, if there is some proof connecting the axioms $\Gamma_{\text{Set}}$ to $S$ using the deduction rules.
			
			The goal of this monograph is to develop \gls{lfol} and \gls{fol} so that we can begin making statements and proofs within set theory.
			However, this development will be a great labor.
			One might initially hope that we can lay out \gls{lfol} and \gls{fol} without using any mathematics at all since we are hoping to \textit{use} these structures to setup a foundation for mathematics.
			Perhaps we could lay out \gls{lfol} and \gls{fol} using only natural language, logic, and other patently obvious concepts so that there is no fear of circularity in this foundations project.
			However, we will find that this is entirely impossible.
			\gls{lfol} and \gls{fol} will depend heavily on what we will refer to as \textbf{preformal} mathematical concepts.
			The simplest example is that our alphabet for \gls{lfol} will include an infinite number of predicate and function symbols which will be enumerated both by an index number $n$ and an arity $\nu$.
			Such a labeling scheme presupposes an understanding of the natural, or counting, numbers.
			This tells us that for a person to understand \gls{lfol} they must already understand the counting numbers even though we hope to eventually \textbf{formally} define the natural numbers $\N$ using formal statements written in \gls{lfol} as an object within set theory.
			The counting numbers are one of the simplest preformal concepts we will find necessary for our development. 
			The  most sophisticated preformal concepts we will require are structural induction and recursion.
			We will find that the syntax of \gls{lfol} is very naturally expressed using structural induction and we will also find the need, when developing \gls{lfol} and \gls{fol} to make structurally recursive definitions leaning on the structural inductive construction of \gls{lfol}.
			We will go through a very technical and rigorous, yet still preformal, investigation of these concepts so that we can proceed with our development of \gls{lfol} and \gls{fol}.
			
			This treatment, then, addresses an interesting philosophical question related to circularity within the foundations of mathematics.
			The question is how much \textbf{preformal} mathematical machinery is necessary to develop \textbf{formal} mathematics.
			As mentioned in the previous paragraph, one would hope the set of preformal mathematics needed is small but, as we will see, there are actually a large number of preformal mathematical definitions and theorems we will require.
			I will also note that just because we discuss a preformal concept does \textbf{not} mean the discussion is not rigorous or technically difficult. 
			The preformal proof, for example, of the structural recursion theorem will look very much like a rigorous and non-trivial mathematical proof.
			
		\section{A Note on Syntax and Semantics}
			
			Two critical pillars within formal logic are syntax and semantics.
			Syntax encapsulates the formal rules described above regarding the alphabet of a formal language, the syntactic rules for combining symbols into formulas, and the inference rules for deriving new formulas from old.
			In short, syntax describes the \qq{rules of the game} of formal logic.
			Semantics is, crudely, the assigning of meaning to the symbols of the language.
			In its simplest form, semantics is the assigning of truth values to formulas.
			
			Let's consider an example.
			Suppose we would like to formalize the statement: \qq{If $m$ is a natural number and $m$ is odd then $2\times m$ is odd}.
			In \gls{lfol} this expression might be formalized as
			\begin{equation*}
				\forall m\implies \in m \mathbb{N} \land Om O\times 2 m
			\end{equation*}
			using prefix notation or
			\begin{equation*}
				\forall m ( (m \in \N \land O(m)) \implies O(2\times m))
			\end{equation*}
			using infix notation.
			While infix notation is better for human readability, we will prefer prefix notation in most cases in this monograph because making making proofs about \gls{lfol} will be easier if we using prefix notation.
			
			The smallest element in our formal language is the \textit{term}.
			A term is one of three things: (1) a concrete object such as the number \qq{2}, (2) a variable into which concrete objects can be \qq{plugged in} such as \qq{$m$}, or (3) an $n$-ary function of other terms such as \qq{$\div m 2$}.
			The terms in the above expression are
			\begin{equation*}
				m, \N, m, \times 2 m, 2, m
			\end{equation*}
			Above I've included every term in the formula as it appears from left to right including repetitions of the same term.
			Note that the first occurence of $m$ to the right of $\forall$ is not serving the syntactic role of a term but is rather part of the \qq{for all} quantification.
			The only variable which appears is \qq{$m$}.
			We see that \qq{$2$} and \qq{$\mathbb{N}$} appear as constants.
			We see that \qq{$\times$} appears as a 2-ary function with arguments \qq{$m$} and \qq{2} which are a variable and constant, respectively.
			
			The next larger element in our formal language is the \textit{atomic formula}.
			An atomic formula is the combination of an $n$-ary predicate symbol and $n$ terms.
			We have two predicate symbols in the above formula: \qq{$\in$} is a 2-ary predicate and \qq{$O$} is a 1-ary predicate symbol.
			The atomic formulas are then
			\begin{align*}
				&\in m \mathbb{N}\\
				&Om\\
				&O\times 2 m
			\end{align*}
			
			Finally, the next larger object in our language is the formula.
			Atomic formulas themselves are already formulas, but we can form larger, more complex, formulas by stringing together smaller formulas with connectives such as \qq{$\land$} and \qq{$\implies$} or quantifying with \qq{$\forall$}.
			The formulas in the above expression are
			\begin{align*}
				&\forall m\implies \in m \mathbb{N} \land Om O\times 2 m\\
				&\implies \in m \mathbb{N} \land Om O\times 2 m\\
				&\in m\mathbb{N} \land Om\\
				&\in m \mathbb{N}\\
				&Om\\
				&O\times 2 m
			\end{align*}
			
			The preceding discussion within this section has been a purely syntactic analysis of the formula in question.
			That is, we broke the formula down into smaller parts within the formal language according to the syntax rules of the language.
			In the following sections we will learn that the formula given above is indeed a valid formula within \gls{lfol}.
			
			Semantics enters the discussion when we give an \textit{interpretation} to the various expressions above.
			For example, when we \textit{interpret} the symbol \qq{2} as the number 2, the expression \qq{$\times 2 m$} as the multiplication of the number \qq{2} with the variable \qq{$m$}, or the predicate \qq{$Om$} to mean that the variable $m$ is odd, we are imposing semantics onto the expression.
			We also are making a semantic interpretation any time we impose or deduce a truth value for a given formula.
			For example, on all of the usual interpretations, we would assign a value of \qq{false} to the formula above because twice an odd number is \textit{not} an odd number.
			While we require semantics to assign a truth value to a statement such as the one above, we only require syntax and the deduction rules to search for a formal proof of such a statement and, in fact, for the expression above, we would be able to provide a proof for the negation of that expression given the set theory axioms and definitions of the corresponding predicates and terms.
			
			This document leans towards a game formalism philosophical approach to formal logic.
			Game formalism is a philosophical view on the foundations of mathematics which holds that it is possible to describe set theory and, by extension, all of mathematics, using only syntactic manipulations within \gls{lfol} and \gls{fol}.
			Or, more conservatively, at the very least, game formalism asks the question of how far one can get using such an approach.
			To that end, I would like it to be clear that all definitions and arguments should be self-contained within purely syntactic analyses.
			At a few times, I may make reference to semantic ideas, but this will only be to motivate and give intuition for syntactic definitions and manipulations.
			Such references should be disposable with respect to the main focus of this work.
			One could, instead, proceed by dropping semantics entirely from the narrative.
			In such an approach, some definitions may appear unmotivated, at least at first, but that is not, technically, a problem for the game formalist.

	\chapter{Basic Preformal Concepts}
		\label{ch:preformalbasic}
		One goal of this work is to carefully define \gls{lfol} and \gls{fol} so that we can eventually formally describe set theory with \gls{fol} to serve as a foundation for the formalization of much of modern mathematics.
		
		However, we will not be able to perform this task starting from nothing.
		Indeed, to create this foundation for formal mathematics, we will find ourselves relying on \textbf{preformal} conceptions of various simple mathematical constructs such as the natural numbers.
		
		In one sense, the definitions and proofs which appear about preformal concepts should be thought of like definitions of English words which are understood by speakers and listeners of the language.
		However, some definitions and proof we will encounter will be technical and will closely or exactly mirror their formal counterparts within formal mathematics.
		From this we take the understanding that we actually require some surprisingly powerful preformal concepts to lay out the foundations for formal mathematics.
		
		\section{Preformal Logic}
	
			\begin{preformal definition}[Logic]
				To build up our formal system of logic we will indeed require a preformal system of logic.
				It is necessary that we preformally or intuitively understand concepts like
				\begin{itemize}
					\item{If $\mc{A}$ is true and $\mc{B}$ is true then \qq{$\mc{A}$ and $\mc{B}$} is true.}
					\item{If $\mc{A}$ implies $\mc{C}$ and $\mc{B}$ implies $\mc{C}$ then if \qq{$\mc{A}$ or $\mc{B}$} is true then $\mc{C}$ is true.}
				\end{itemize}
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Object]
				An object is a clearly definable and delineated entity.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Symbol]
				A symbols is a type of object.
				Written symbols are specific two-dimensional patterns depicted on two-dimensional surfaces like paper or screens.
				We will typically use symbols from the English alphabet and numeral system as well as some punctuation symbols and brackets.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Object Equality]
				Suppose the symbols $x$, $y$, and $z$ represent objects.
				If $x$ and $y$ are the same object then we may write $x = y$. Otherwise we write $x\not = y$.
				From this it (preformally) follows that if $x= y$ then $y = x$ and that if $x = y$ and $y = z$ then $x = z$.
				We of course also always have that $x = x$.
			\end{preformal definition}		
		
		\section{Preformal Set Theory}
		
			\begin{preformal definition}[Set]
				A set is an object which contains other objects.
				if $S$ is a set, $x$ is an object, and $S$ contains $x$ then we write $x \in S$.
				We write
				\begin{equation*}
					S = \{x, y, z\}
				\end{equation*}
				to indicate $x\in S$, $y\in S$, $z\in S$ and there is no other object in $S$ not equal to one of $x$, $y$, or $z$.
				In other words, if $w\in S$ then $w=x$, $w=y$, or $w=z$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Subset]
				If $S$ and $T$ are sets and all elements that are contained in $S$ are also contained in $T$ then we say $S$ is a subset of $T$ and we denote this by $S \subset T$.
				This can also be more rigorously by specifying that $S\subset T$ if for all $x\in S$ we have that $x\in T$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Set Equality]
				Two sets $S$ and $T$ are equal if they contain the same elements.
				This is equivalent to the statement that $S\subset T$ and $T\subset S$.
				The former statement says that every element of $S$ is in $T$ and the latter says every element of $T$ is in $S$.
				We denote this by $S=T$.
				
				From this definition it follows that
				\begin{itemize}
					\item{$S=S$}
					\item{$S=T$ implies $T=S$}
					\item{If $U$ is a third set, then if $S=T$ and $T=U$ then $S=U$.}
				\end{itemize}
				
				Consider $S = \{x\}$ and $S' = \{x, y\}$.
				But suppose that $y = x$.
				In this case we can see that $S' \subset S$ still and $S \subset S'$ so $\{x\} = S = S' = \{x, y\}$.
				That is, a set only contains each unique element once. 
				There is no distinction between a set containing multiple copies of the same element and the same set containing just one copy of that element.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[The Empty Set]
				There is a set which contains no elements which we denote by $\emptyset = \{\}$.
				For any object $x$, we know that $x\not \in \emptyset$.
				Notably, the empty set is a subset of any other set.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Subset Specification]
				Suppose $S$ is a set and $\phi(s)$ is a logical expression that depends on a logical variable $s$. 		
				We can then extract a subset of $S$ by picking out all of the elements $s\in S$ such that $\phi(s)$.
				
				This specification set is denoted by
				\begin{equation*}
					\{s\in S \mid \phi(s)\}
				\end{equation*}
				
				Now suppose we have a list of logical variables $\bv{t}$.
				Suppose also the expression $\phi(\bv{t})$ now depends on this list.
				Suppose also we have a logical function $f(\bv{t})$ that results in a new logical object depending on the list of logical objects inserted into $\bv{t}$. 
				We can then perform a more sophisticated type of specification given by
				\begin{align*}
					\{f(\bv{t})\in S \mid \phi(\bv{t})\} = \{s\in S \mid \text{ there exists } \bv{t} \text{ such that } s = f(\bv{t}) \text{ and } \phi(\bv{t})\}
				\end{align*}
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			Sets constructed using subset specification are always subsets of a larger set $S$.
			If we were to drop the constraint that the specification set is a subset of a larger set $S$ and allow sets of the form $\{x \mid \phi(x)\}$, i.e. to allow all objects $x$ satisfying a certain property $\phi(x)$ to form a set, then we will run into the Russel paradox which we describe now.
			
			The Russell paradox considers the Russell \qq{set}
			\begin{equation*}
				R = \{s \mid s\not \in s\}
			\end{equation*}
			The paradox arises when we ask the question \qq{is $R\in R$}. 
			If we assume $R\in R$ then, by looking at the definition of $R$, we see that we must have $R\not \in R$, a contradiction.
			If we assume $R\not \in R$ then, again, by looking at the definition of $R$, we find that $R \in R$, again, a contradiction.
			We see a contradiction whether $R\in R$ or $R\not \in R$.
			The Russel set exposes a major flaw if we allow \textbf{unrestricted} set specification.
			By using \textbf{restricted} subset specification, in which we must always be selecting our specified set out of an initial set $S$, we avoid Russel's paradox.
			
			Another type of pathology we must avoid in formal set theory is that of infinite descending sequences of sets.
			For this, suppose we had \qq{sets} $s$ and $t$ which satisfied $s\in t$ and $t\in s$.
			Then we would have $t = \{s\} = \{\{t\}\} = \{\{\{s\}\} = \{\ldots \{s\}\ldots \}$.
			In \gls{zf} set theory, we avoid such constructions using a specific axiom that forbids these \qq{rregular} sets.
			In our preformal setting we will not explicitly address this pathology.
			Rather, we will just only consider simple enough objects that we know this isn't of concern to the preformal theory.
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Set Union]
				If $S$ is a collection of sets.
				We can take the union over the elements of $S$.
				This set, denoted by $\bigcup S$, contains all the elements contained in the elements of $S$.
				That is, $x \in \bigcup S$ if and only if there is a $y\in S$ with $x\in y$.
				If $S$ contains two elements $T$ and $U$ then we write
				$$
				\bigcup S = \bigcup \{T, U\} = T \cup U
				$$
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Set Intersection]
				If $S$ is a collection of sets then we define the intersection of the elements of $S$ to be the set whose elements are exactly those which are contained in each element of $S$.
				$$
				\bigcap S = \{x \in \bigcup S \mid \text{For each } y \in S \text{ we have } x \in y\}
				$$
				If $S$ has two elements so that $S = \{T, U\}$ then we write
				$$
				\bigcap S = \bigcap \{T, U\} = T \cap U
				$$
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Set Subtraction]
				If $S$ and $T$ are sets then we may subtract $T$ from $S$ by collecting all the elements of $S$ which are not in $T$:
				\begin{equation*}
					S\setminus T = \{x \in S \mid x \not \in T\}
				\end{equation*}
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{definition}[Power Set]
				If $S$ is a set then we denote the set of all subsets of $S$ by $\mc{P}(S)$.
				Note that $\emptyset, S \in \mc{P}(S)$.
			\end{definition}			
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Ordered Pair]
				If $x$ and $y$ are objects then we create a new object called the ordered pair of $x$ and $y$ and denote it by $\braket{x, y}$.
				Ordered pairs have the property that $\braket{x, y} = \braket{x', y'}$ if and only if $x' = x$ and $y' = y$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Cartesian Product]
				If $X$ and $Y$ are two sets then we may construct a set which consists of all the ordered pairs whose first elements are contained in $X$ and whose second elements are contained in $Y$.
				This set is called the Cartesian product of $X$ and $Y$ and is denoted by $X\times Y$.
				We have that $\braket{x, y} \in X\times Y$ if and only if $x \in X$ and $y \in Y$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Relation]
				If $X$ and $Y$ are two sets and $R\subset X\times Y$ then we say $R$ is a relation between $X$ and $Y$.
				If $\braket{x, y} \in R$ then we say $x$ and $y$ are related by $R$.

				Note that there may by $X', Y'$ possibly with $X' \neq X$ and $Y' \neq Y$ such that $R\subset X' \times Y'$. 
				So, that means that it is not uniquely determined which two sets between which $R$ is a relation.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Domain and Range]
				Suppose $R$ is a relation between $X$ and $Y$ so that $R\subset X \times Y$.
				We define
				\begin{align*}
					\text{Dom}(R) =& \{x \in X \mid \text{There exist } y \in Y \text{ such that } \braket{x, y} \in R\}\\
					\text{Ran}(R) =& \{y \in Y \mid \text{There exist } x \in X \text{ such that } \braket{x, y} \in R\}
				\end{align*}
				
				If $R\subset X \times Y$ then $\text{Dom}(R)\subset X$ and $\text{Ran}(R)\subset Y$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Image Under a Relation]
				If $R$ is a relation then for a set $X$, we define
				\begin{equation*}
					R_{\text{Img}}(X) = \{y \in \text{Ran}(R) \mid \text{There exists} x\in X \text{ and } \braket{x, y} \in R\}
				\end{equation*}
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Functional Relation]
				Suppose $R\subset X \times Y$.
				If $\braket{x, y}, \braket{x, y'}\in R$ implies that $y=y'$ then we say $R$ is a functional relation.
				If $R$ is a functional relation and $x\in \text{Dom}(R)$ then there is a unique $y\in \text{Ran}(R)$ such that $\braket{x, y} \in R$.
				We denote this unique $y$ by $R(x)$.
				We may simply call a functional relation a function.
				Note that $R_{\text{Img}}(\{x\}) = \{R(x)\}$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Injective Relation]
				Suppose $R\subset X \times Y$.
				If $\braket{x, y}, \braket{x', y} \in R$ implies $x=x'$ then $R$ we say $R$ is an injective relation.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Inverse Relation]
				Suppose $R\subset X\times Y$.
				We define the inverse relation $R^{-1}$ by
				\begin{align*}
					R^{-1} = \{\braket{y, x}\in Y\times X \mid \braket{x, y}\in R\}
				\end{align*}
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal theorem}[Relation Inverse Swaps Domain and Range]
				We will prove 
				\begin{itemize}
					\item{$\text{Dom}(R^{-1}) = \text{Ran}(R)$}
					\item{$\text{Ran}(R^{-1}) = \text{Dom}(R)$}
				\end{itemize}
				Suppose $y\in \text{Dom}(R^{-1})$.
				That means there exists $x\in X$ such that $\braket{y, x}\in R^{-1}$ which means $\braket{x, y}\in R$. But that means $y\in \text{Ran}(R)$ so $\text{Dom}(R^{-1})\subset \text{Ran}(R)$.
				Now suppose $y\in \text{Ran}(R)$ which means there exists $x\in X$ with $\braket{x, y}\in R$. 
				But that means $\braket{y, x}\in R^{-1}$ which means $y\in \text{Dom}(R^{-1})$. So we conclude $\text{Ran}(R)\subset \text{Dom}(R^{-1})$) which means $\text{Ran}(R) = \text{Dom}(R^{-1})$.
				
				Now suppose $x\in \text{Ran}(R^{-1})$ so that there exist $y\in Y$ such that $\braket{y, x}\in R^{-1}$.
				This means $\braket{x, y} \in R$ which means $x\in \text{Dom}(R)$ so $\text{Ran}(R^{-1}) \subset \text{Dom}(R)$.
				Now suppose $x\in \text{Dom}(R)$. 
				Then there exists $y\in Y$ with $\braket{x, y}\in R$, but that means $\braket{y, x}\in R^{-1}$ so that $y\in \text{Ran}(R^{-1})$.
				This means $\text{Dom}(R) \subset \text{Ran}(R^{-1})$ so we conclude $\text{Ran}(R^{-1}) = \text{Dom}(R)$.		
			\end{preformal theorem}	
			
			\begin{preformal theorem}[Functional Implies Injective Inverse]
				If $R\subset X\times Y$ is functional then $R^{-1}$ is injective.
				Suppose $\braket{y, x}, \braket{y',x}\in R^{-1}$.
				Then $\braket{x, y}, \braket{x, y'}\in R$, but $R$ is functional which implies $y=y'$. 
				But this means $R^{-1}$ is injective.
			\end{preformal theorem}
			
			\begin{preformal theorem}[Injective Implies Functional Inverse]
				If $R\subset X\times Y$ is injective that $R^{-1}$ is functional.
				Suppose $(y, x), (y, x')\in R^{-1}$.
				Then $(x, y), (x', y) \in R$, but $R$ is injective, so this means $x=x'$.
				But that means $R^{-1}$ is functional.
				
				Therefore, if a functional relation is injective, then we say it is invertible because the inverse relation is also functional.
			\end{preformal theorem}
						
			\begin{preformal definition}[Relation Restriction]
				Suppose $R$ is a relation between $X$ and $Y$ and $X'$ is a set.
				We define a new relation
				$$
				R|_{X'} = \{\braket{x', y} \in R \mid x' \in X'\}
				$$
				$R|_{X'}$ is the restriction of $R$ to the set $(X'\cap X) \times Y \subset X \times Y$.
				
				Often, but not always, $X' \subset X$ so that $X' \cap X = X'$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[The Natural Numbers]
				We require a preformal understanding of the natural numbers.
				These are the counting numbers $0, 1, 2, \ldots$. 
				We denote the set of all natural numbers by $\N$.
				We assume an understanding of equality $=$ between natural numbers and of the comparison operations $<, \le, \ge, >$.
				We also assume an understanding of the binary addition operator $+$.
				We let $\N^+ = \N \setminus\{0\}$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[The set 1 through $n$ (inclusive)]
				We define
				\begin{equation*}
					[n] = \{m\in \N \mid 1 \le m \le n\}.
				\end{equation*}
				So, for example,
				\begin{equation*}
					[3] = \{1, 2, 3\}
				\end{equation*}
				Note that this notation uses 1-indexing rather than 0-indexing.
				We allow $[0]$ but note that $[0]$ evaluates to $[0] = \emptyset$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Tuple]
				An $I$-tuple is a functional relation $f$ with $\text{Dom}(f) = I$.
				We often notate the tuple in bold face, e.g. $\bv{t}$, and, for $i\in I$, notate the entries in the tuple with subscript notation:
				\begin{equation*}
					\bv{t}(i) = t_i
				\end{equation*}
				When defining the tuple we may write
				\begin{equation*}
					\bv{t} = (t_i)_{i\in I}.
				\end{equation*}
				If we are writing all the entries in the tuple explicitly we write, e.g.,
				\begin{equation*}
					\bv{t} = (t_1, t_2, t_3)
				\end{equation*}
				using parentheses as brackets.
				
				Typically $I = [n]$ for some $n \in \N$ in which case we call the tuple an $n$-tuple. 
				
				If $I = [0] = \emptyset$ and $\bv{t}$ is an $I$ tuple then $\bv{t}$ is a functional relation with $\text{Dom}(\bv{t}) = \emptyset$.
				The only relation with domain equal to the $\emptyset$ is $\emptyset$.
				Suppose $R$ is a relation and $\text{Dom}(R) = \emptyset$.
				Then consider $\braket{x, y} \in R$.
				This means $x \in \emptyset$, but there is no object contained in the empty set so therefor there is no ordered pair contained in $R$.
				So this means there is exactly one $I=[0]=\emptyset$ tuple.
				We denote this tuple by
				\begin{equation}
					\varnothing = () = \emptyset.
				\end{equation}
				$\varnothing$ is simply a different font for $\emptyset$, but we will use $\varnothing$ specifically to refer to the unique 0-tuple noting that, as sets, $\varnothing$ and $\emptyset$ happen to be equal because of how we have defined tuple in terms of sets.
				
				If we want to emphasize that the elements within the tuple are sets we may call an $I$-tuple an $I$-indexed family.
			\end{preformal definition}

			\begin{preformal definition}[Set Operations on Tuples]
				Suppose $\bv{S} = (S_i)_{i\in I}$.
				We define
				\begin{align*}
					\bigcup_{i\in I} S_i =& \bigcup \text{Ran}(\bv{S})\\
					\bigcap_{i\in I} S_i =& \bigcap \text{Ran}(\bv{S}).
				\end{align*}
				if $I = [n]$ then we define
				\begin{align*}
					\bigcup_{i=1}^n S_i =& \bigcup_{i\in [n]} S_i = \bigcup \text{Ran}(\bv{S})\\
					\bigcap_{i=1}^n S_i =& \bigcap_{i\in [n]} S_i = \bigcap \text{Ran}(\bv{S})\\
				\end{align*}
			\end{preformal definition}
		
			\begin{preformal definition}[Relation Tuple]
				A relation tuple is a 3-tuple of the form $(X, Y, R)$ where $R\subset X\times Y$ is a relation.
				$X$ is called the source and $Y$ is called the target.
				Note that $\text{Dom}(R) \subset X$ and $\text{Ran}(R) \subset Y$. 
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Total and Surjective]
				If $(X, Y, R)$ is a relation tuple then we say the relation (tuple) is total if $\text{Dom}(R) = X$ and surjective if $\text{Ran}(R) = Y$.
				Note that total and surjective only make sense in the context of a relation \textit{tuple} and not in the case of a bare relation. 
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Function Tuple]
				If $(X, Y, f)$ is a total relation tuple and $f$ is a functional relation then we say $(X, Y, f)$ is a function tuple.
				We denote this by $f:X\to Y$.
				
				From the notation $f: X\to Y$ we are meant to infer that $f\subset X\times Y$, $f$ is functional, $\text{Dom}(f) = X$.
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[All Functions from $X$ to $Y$]
				Suppose $f:X\to Y$ then $f\subset X\times Y$. 
				This means $f\in \mc{P}(X\times Y)$.
				We can, then, define the set of all functions with domain $X$ and target $Y$.
				$$
				\text{Func}(X, Y) = Y^X = \{f \in \mc{P}(X\times Y) \mid f: X \to Y\}
				$$				
			\end{preformal definition}
			
			\begin{preformal definition}[Cartesian Projection Functions]
				We define
				\begin{align*}
					\pi_1: X\times Y \to& X\\
					\braket{x, y}\mapsto& x\\
					\pi_2: X\times Y \to & Y\\
					\braket{x, y} \mapsto & y
				\end{align*}
				One can confirm that $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ thus defined are total, surjective, and functional relations.
				However, they are not injective and, therefore, the corresponding inverse relations are not functional.
			\end{preformal definition}
	
			\begin{preformal definition}[Multiple Cartesian Product]
				Suppose $\bv{S}$ is an $I$-tuple of sets.
				We define the multiple cartesian product as
				\begin{equation*}
					\bigtimes \mathcal{S} = \{\bv{s} \in \bv{S}^I \mid \text{for each } i \in I, s_i \in S_i\}
				\end{equation*}				
				In other words, it is the set of $I$-tuples such that the $i^{\text{th}}$ entry is in the $i^{\text{th}}$ entry of $\bv{S}$.
				
				We can also notate this as
				\begin{equation*}
					\bigtimes_{i\in I} S_i = \bigtimes \bv{S}
				\end{equation*}
				or if $I=[n]$ then
				\begin{equation*}
					S_1 \times \ldots \times S_n = \bigtimes_{i=1}^n S_i = \bigtimes_{i\in I} S_i = \bigtimes \bv{S}
				\end{equation*}
				
				If $\bv{s} \in S_1 \times \ldots \times S_n$ then we may write
				\begin{equation*}
					\bv{s} = (s_1, \ldots, s_n) = s_1\times \ldots \times s_n \in S_1 \times \ldots \times S_n.
				\end{equation*}
				
				If $I=[n]$ and $S_i = S$ for each $i\in [n]$ then we let
				\begin{align*}
					S^n = \bigtimes_{i=1}^n S_i
				\end{align*}
				
				Note that there is a natural identification between $S$ and $S^1$ given by $s \mapsto (s)$.
				Sometimes it will be convenient for us to abuse notation and identify with $S$ with $S^1$ and $s$ with $(s)$.
				Note that $S^0 = \{()\}$.
			\end{preformal definition}
	
			\begin{preformal definition}[Function Whose Domain is a Cartesian Product]
				Suppose $\bv{S}$ is an $n$-tuple of sets, suppose $f: \bv{S} \to T$, and suppose 
				\begin{equation*}
					\bv{s} = (s_1, \ldots, s_n).
				\end{equation*}
				We then write
				\begin{equation*}
					f(s_1, \ldots, s_n) = f((s_1, \ldots, s_n))
				\end{equation*}
				noting that, on the left, we have elided one otherwise necessary pair of parentheses for better legibility.
			\end{preformal definition}
	
			\begin{preformal definition}[Function Whose Range is a Cartesian Product]
				Suppose $\bv{S}$ is an $n$-tuple of sets and suppose $f:T \to \bv{S}$.
				If $t\in T$ and $f(t) = \bv{s} \in \bv{S}$.
				Then we use the notations
				\begin{equation*}
					\bv{s}_i = (f(t))(i) = f(t)(i) = (f(t))_i = f_i(t)
				\end{equation*}
			\end{preformal definition}
	
			\begin{preformal definition}[Bijection]
				If $f$ is an invertible function then we say $f$ is a bijection between $\text{Dom}(f)$ and $\text{Ran}(f)$.
			\end{preformal definition}
	
			\begin{preformal definition}[Lists]
				Suppose $S$ is a set.
				We define the set of $S$ lists by
				\begin{equation*}
					S^* = \bigcup_{n\in \N} S^n
				\end{equation*}
				We define a function 
				\begin{align*}
					\text{Len} = \{\braket{\bv{s}, n} \in S^* \times \N \mid \bv{s}: [n] \to S\}
				\end{align*}
				$\text{Len}$ is total on $S^*$ and functional because for each $\bv{s} \in S^*$ there is exactly one $n\in \N$ such that $\bv{s}: [n] \to S$.
				We may also denote $\text{Len}(\bv{s}) = \norm{\bv{s}}$
				We can see that $\bv{s}:[\norm{\bv{s}}] \to S$.
				
				We will sometimes have use for
				\begin{equation*}
					S^{>0} = S^*\setminus S^0
				\end{equation*}
			\end{preformal definition}
	
			\begin{preformal definition}[Function Mapping over Cartesian Product]
				Suppose $f:X \to Y$ and $n\in \mathbb{N}$.
				We define
				\begin{align}
					f^{(n)} = \{\braket{\bv{x}, \bv{y}} \in X^n \times Y^n \mid \text{for each } i \in [n], y_i = f(x_i)\}
				\end{align}
				
				We see that $f^{(n)}$ is a relation between $X^n$ and $Y^n$.
				Indeed, there is always a unique $\bv{y}$ for each $\bv{x}$ satisfying $y_i = f(x_i)$ which means $f^{(n)}$ is a total and functional relation which means we can regard it as a function.
			\end{preformal definition}
	
	\chapter{Preformal Structural Induction and Recursion}
	\label{ch:preformalinduction}

		We now proceed into the study of preformal structural induction and recursion.
	
		\begin{preformal definition}[$U$ Function]
			Suppose $U$ is a set, $U' \subset U$ and $f: (U')^{\nu} \to U$.
			Then we say $f$ is a $\nu$-ary $U$ function.
		\end{preformal definition}
	
		\begin{preformal definition}[Closed Under a  $U$ Function]
			Suppose $f$ is a $\nu$-ary $U$ function.
			If $\text{Ran}(f|_{(U')^{\nu}}) \subset U'$ then we say $U'$ is closed under $f$.
		\end{preformal definition}
	
		\begin{preformal definition}[$U$ Function Family]
			Suppose $U$ and $I$ are sets.
			Suppose $\bv{\nu} = (\nu_i)_{i\in I}$, $\bv{U}' = (U_i)_{i\in I}$ and $\bv{f} = (f_i)_{i\in I}$ are all $I$-tuples.
			Suppose, for all $i \in I$ that $\nu_i \in \N^+$, $U'_i \subset U$ and $f_i:(U'_i)^{\nu_i} \to U$.
			Then we say $\bv{f}$ is a $\bv{\nu}$-ary $U$ function family.
			We exclude $0$-ary functions from the definition.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Closed Under a $U$ Function Family]
			Suppose $\bv{f} = (f_i)_{i\in I}$ is a $U$ function family.
			We say $U'\subset U$ if $U'$ is closed under $f_i$ for each $i\in I$.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[$U$ Function Family Restriction]
			Suppose $\bv{f}$ is a $\bv{\nu}$-ary $U$ function family and $U' \subset \text{Dom}(f_i)$ for each $i\in I$.
			Then we define the $I$-tuple $\bv{f}|_{U'} = (f_i|_{(U')^{\nu_i}})_{i\in I}$.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[$(U, B, \bv{f})$-Inductive]
			Suppose $U$ is a set ($U$ stands for universe), $B\subset U$ ($B$ stands for base) and $\bv{f}$ is a $U$ function family.
			
			Now suppose $C\subset U$.
			If 
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{$B\subset C$ and}
				\item{$C$ is closed under $\bv{f}|_C$}
			\end{itemize}
			then we say $C$ is $(U, B, \bv{f})$-inductive.
			It follows that $U$ is $(U, B, \bv{f})$-inductive.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[$(B, \bv{f})$ Inductively Generated]
			Suppose $N$ is a set with $B\subset N$ and $\bv{f}$ is an $N$ function family.
			Suppose that any $(N, B, \bv{f})$ inductive subset of $N$ must be equal to $N$.
			Then we say $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		This is equivalent to saying that $N$ is inductively generated if any subset $M\subset N$ containing $B$ and closed under $\bv{f}|_M$ must be equal to $N$.
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Inductively Generated Implies Inductive]
			Suppose $\bv{f} = (f_i)_{i\in I}$ is an $N$ function family and $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
			We know that $B\subset N$ and we know that $\text{Ran}(f_i) \subset N$ for each $i\in I$ because $\bv{f}$ is an $N$ function family.
			But this means $N$ is $(N, B, \bv{f})$ inductive.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[The Induction Principle]
			Suppose $\bv{f}=(f_i)_{i\in I}$ is a $\bv{\nu}$-ary $N$ function family and $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
			Consider an expression $\phi(n)$ which depends on $n\in N$.
			Now consider the set
			\begin{equation*}
				\bar{N} = \{n\in N \mid \phi(n)\}
			\end{equation*}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{Base case: Suppose we can prove $\phi(n)$ holds for each $n\in B$.}
				\item{Inductive Step: Now consider $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i)$. Suppose that, if we assume $\phi(n_j)$ for each $j\in [\nu_i]$, then we can prove $\phi(f_i(\bv{n}))$.}
			\end{itemize}
			The base case tells use that $B \subset \bar{N}$.
			Now consider $i\in I$ and $f_i$.
			We want to show $\bar{N}$ is closed under $f_i|_{\bar{N}^{\nu_i}}$.
			Consider $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i|_{\bar{N}^{\nu_i}}) = \text{Dom}(f_i) \cap \bar{N}^{\nu_i}$.
			But this means $\bv{n} \in \bar{N}^{\nu_i}$ which, for each $j\in [\nu_i]$ that $n_j \in \bar{N}$ which means $\phi(n_j)$ holds.
			But, the induction hypothesis then tells us that $\phi(f_i(\bv{n}))$ holds meaning $f_i(\bv{n}) \in \bar{N}$.
			This means that $\bar{N}$ is closed under $f_i|_{\bar{N}^{\nu_i}}$ and, more generally, $\bar{N}$ is closed under $f|_{\bar{N}}$.
			This means we have proven that $\bar{N}$ is $(N, B, \bv{f})$ inductive.
			But since, $\bar{N}$ is a $(N, B, \bv{f})$ inductive subset of $N$ which is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated, we have that $\bar{N} = N$ which means $\phi(n)$ holds for all $n\in N$.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		The induction principle provides a means to prove some property about all of $N$ by proving that property only for $B$, the base case, and proving that $\bv{f}$ preserves that property in each inductive step.
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[$(U, B, \bv{f})$ Inductive Closure]
			Suppose $\bv{f}$ is a $U$ function family.
			We then define
			\begin{equation*}
				\mc{N}_{U, B, \bv{f}} = \bigcap \{N \in \mc{P}(U) \mid N \text{ is } (U, B, \bv{f})
			\end{equation*}
			\begin{equation*}
				N_{U, B, \bv{f}} = \bigcap \mc{N}_{U, B, \bv{f}}.
			\end{equation*}
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[The Inductive Closure is Inductive]
			Since each $N\in \mc{N}_{U, B, \bv{f}}$ is $(U, B, \bv{f})$ inductive we know that $B \subset N$.
			But this means $B \subset N_{U, B, \bv{f}}$.			
			Now consider $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i|_{N_{U, B, F}}) \subset N_{U, B, \bv{f}}^{\nu_i}$.
			For each $j\in [\nu_i]$ we see that $n_j \in N_{U, B, \bv{f}}$.
			But this means that $n_j \in N$ for each $N \in \mc{N}_{U, B, \bv{f}}$.
			But since each $N$ is $(U, B, \bv{f})$ inductive, this means $f_i(\bv{n}) \in N$ each $N$.
			But this means $f_i(\bv{n}) \in N_{U, B, \bv{f}}$ which implies $N_{U, B, \bv{f}}$ is closed under $\bv{f}$ and is, therefore, $(U, B, \bv{f})$ inductive.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[The Inductive Closure is Inductively Generated]
			Let $N = N_{U, B, \bv{f}}$.
			Now suppose $M\subset N$ is $(N, B, \bv{f}|_N)$ inductive.
			We must show $N \subset M$.
			But, since $N \subset U$, $(N, B, \bv{f}|_N)$ inductive implies $(U, B, \bv{f})$ inductive.
			But if $M$ is $(U, B, \bv{f})$ inductive, then $M\in \mc{N}_{U, B, \bv{f}}$ which means $M\subset N = \bigcap \mc{N}_{U, B, \bv{f}}$.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Each Elemented of an Inductively Generated Set is Base or Generated]
			Suppose $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
			We will show that either $n\in B$ or there is some $i\in I$ such that $n \in \text{Ran}(f_i)$.
			Call this proposition $\phi(n)$.
			
			Consider
			\begin{equation*}
				C = \{n\in N \mid n\in B \text{ or there is } i\in I \text{ such that } n \in \text{Ran}(f_i)\}
			\end{equation*}
			Clearly $B\subset C$.
			Now consider $i \in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i|_C) \subset N^{\nu_i}$.
			Since $f_i(\bv{n}) \in \text{Ran}(f_i)$ we have that $f_i(\bv{n}) \in C$.
			But this means $C$ is closed under $\bv{f}$ which means $C \subset N$ is $(N, B, \bv{f})$ inductive which implies $C = N$ because $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		The last theorem establishes that if $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated than any element $n\in N$ is either an element of $B$ or is in the range of $f_i$ for some $i\in I$.
		However, it leaves open the possibilities that
		\begin{itemize}
			\item{$n$ may be in the range of multiple $f_i$,}
			\item{$n$ may both be an element of $B$ and be in the range of one or more $f_i$,}
			\item{if $n \in \text{Ran}(f_i)$ there may be multiple elements $\bv{n}\in N^{\nu_i}$ such that $n = f_i(\bv{n})$.}
		\end{itemize}
		The most interesting inductively generated sets are those for which there is exactly one way to construct $n$.
		That is, $n\in B$ xor there is a unique $i \in I$ and a unique $\bv{n} \in N^{\nu_i}$ such that $n = f_i(\bv{n})$.
		If $N$ satisfies these properties then we can imagine constructing a unique parse tree for $N$ where each node is labeled either by an element $b\in B$ or by a function $f_i$ for some $i \in I$.
		If a node is labeled by $f_i$ then that node should have $\nu_i$ ordered children nodes.
		If a node is labeled by an element $b\in B$ then we map that node to $b\in N$.
		If a node is labeled by $f_i$ then that node should map to $f_i(\bv{n})$ where $n_j$ for $j \in [\nu_i]$ corresponds to the value that each child node maps to.
		If $N$ does not have these properties then, in general, there is no unique and finite parse tree for each $n\in N$.
		In general we are most interested in inductively generated sets which do satisfy these properties which we refer to unique readability.
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Unique Readability]
			Suppose $\bv{f}=(f_i)_{i\in I}$ is an $N$ functino family and $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
			Suppose also that for each $n\in N$ exactly one of the following conditions holds:
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{$n\in B$,}
				\item{there exists a unique $i\in I$ and a unique $\bv{n} \in N^{\nu_i}$ such that $n = f_i(\bv{n})$.}
			\end{itemize}
			In this case we say $N$ exhibits unique readability.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Freely Inductively Generated]
			Suppose $\bv{f}=(f_i)_{i\in I}$ is an $N$ function family and $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
			Suppose also that 
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{For each $i \in I$, $f_i$ is injective}
				\item{For each $i\in I$, $\text{Ran}(f_i) \cap B = \emptyset$}
				\item{For any $i, j \in I$ with $i \neq j$, $\text{Ran}(f_i) \cap \text{Ran}(f_j) = \emptyset$}
			\end{itemize}
			If these conditions are true, that range of all $f_i$ is disjoint from $B$ and any other $f_j$, then way $N$ is freely $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Freely Inductively Generated Implies Unique Readability]
			Suppose $\bv{f} = (f_i)_{i\in I}$ is an $N$ function family and $N$ is freely $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
			Then $N$ exhibits unique readability.
			
			If $n\in B$ then by the second freely inductively generated condition we know it is not in the range of $f_i$ for any $i\in I$.
			If $n \not \in B$ then we know from the previous generation theorem that there is some $i\in I$ such that $n\in \text{Ran}(f_i)$.
			But by the first freely inductively generated condition we know that $f_i$ is injective so that there is a unique $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ such that $n = f_i(\bv{n})$ and by third freely inductively generated condition we know that there is no other $j\in I$ with $j\neq i$ such that $n\in \text{Ran}(f_j)$.
		\end{preformal theorem}
	
		The main reason we care most about inductively generated sets which exhibit unique readability is because these are the sets on which we can define functions using recursion.
		A recursive function define on an inductively generated set $N$ which exhibits unique readability is a function $h: N \to S$ where $h|_B$ is specified explicitly via a function $h_B: B \to S$ and a rule is specified which allows us to calculate, for each $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$, the value of $h(f_i(\bv{n}))$ given known values $h(n_j)$ for each $j\in [\nu_i]$.
		For example, the factorial function $\text{Factorial}: \N\to \N$ is defined by
		\begin{align*}
			\text{Factorial}(0) =& 1\\
			\text{Factorial}(n+1) =& (n+1) \times \text{Factorial}(n).
		\end{align*}
		Here the base set is the set containing 0 and the only induction function is that \qq{add one} function.
		We have an intuitive sense that this function is well-defined, however, to prove that such functions can be defined, in general, on structurally inductive sets requires the full power of the recursion theorem below.
		
		We can see why unique readability is necessary for recursion. Suppose, for example, that $f_i(\bv{n}) = b \in B$. 
		Then we have two rules for calculating $h(f_i(\bv{n}))$.
		On the one hand, we have a rule for computing it from $h(n_j)$ for each $j\in [\nu_i]$, but on the other hand, we can calculate it directly via $h_B(f_i(\bv{n}))$.
		There is no particular reason that these two computation should agree.
		The same issue arises if $f_i(\bv{n}) = f_{i'}(\bv{n}')$ for $i'\neq i$ and $\bv{n}' \in \text{Dom}(f_{i'}) \subset N^{\nu_{i'}}$.
		However, this complication is exactly what is avoided by unique readability.
	
		\begin{preformal theorem}[The Structural Recursion Theorem]
			Suppose $\bv{f}=(f_i)_{i\in I}$ is a $\bv{\nu}$-ary $N$ function family and $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated and exhibits unique readability. 
			Suppose that there is a set $S$ and a function $h_B: B \to S$.
			Suppose also that for each $i \in I$ there is a function $h_{f_i}: S^{\nu_i} \to S$.
			
			Under these conditions, the structural recursion theorem states that there is a unique function $h: N \to S$ which satisfies
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{$h|_B = h_B$}
				\item{For each $i\in I$ and for each $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$, we have that $h(f_i(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(h^{(\nu_i)}(\bv{n}))$.}
			\end{itemize}
			
			We define
			\begin{align*}
				\mc{H} = \{&H \in \mc{P}(N\times S) \mid H \text{ is functional and }\\
				&H|_B = h_B \text{ and}\\
				&\text{for all } i \in I \text{ and } \bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset  N^{\nu_i},\\
				&\text{that if } f_i(\bv{n}) \in \text{Dom}(H) \text{ then }\\
				&n_j \in \text{Dom}(H) \text{ for each } j \in [\nu_i] \text{ and }\\
				&H(f_i(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(H^{(\nu_i)}(\bv{n}))\}
			\end{align*}
			and take
			\begin{equation*}
				h = \bigcup \mc{H}.
			\end{equation*}
			
			We now must prove $h$ is total on $N$, functional, satisifes the desired properties, and is unique.
			
			We first prove that if $H_1, H_2 \in \mc{H}$ then $H_1$ and $H_2$ agree on their common domain $D = \text{Dom}(H_1) \cap \text{Dom}(H_2)$.
			We prove this by induction on $N$.
			Specifically, we prove that if $n\in D$ then $H_1(n) = H_2(n)$.
			For the base case if $n\in B$ we know that $H_1(n) = H_2(n) = h_B(n)$.
			For the induction case we consider $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i)\subset  N^{\nu_i}$.
			We assume for each $j\in [\nu_i]$ that if $n_j \in D$ that $H_1(n_j) = H_2(n_j)$.
			If $f_i(\bv{n}) \not \in D$ then there is nothing to prove.
			If $f_i(\bv{n}) \in D$ then we know $n_j \in D$ for each $j \in [\nu_i]$. 
			But we know that $H_1$ and $H_2$ agree on each $n_j$.
			But we also know that
			\begin{equation*}
				H_1(f_i(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(H_1^{(\nu_i)}(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(H_2^{(\nu_i)}(\bv{n})) = H_2(f_i(\bv{n}))
			\end{equation*}
			where the middle equality follows because $H_1$ and $H_2$ agree on the $n_j$ and the outer equalities follow from the condition that $H_1, H_2 \in \mc{H}$.
			But, since all pairs $H_1, H_2 \in \mc{H}$ agree on their common domains, this means $h = \bigcup \mc{H}$ is functional.
			
			We prove by induction that $h$ is total.
			We know that $H|_B = h_B$ for each $H \in B$ so this means $h|_B = h_B$.
			Now consider $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset  N^{\nu_i}$ and assume that $n_j \in \text{Dom}(h)$ for each $j \in [\nu_i]$.
			We must show that $f_i(\bv{n}) \in \text{Dom}(h)$.
			Because $n_j \in \text{Dom}(h)$, this means there is an $H_j \in \mc{H}$ with $n_j \in \text{Dom}(H_j)$.
			Consider the set 
			\begin{align*}
				H = \bigcup_{j\in [\nu_i]} H_j.
			\end{align*}
			We have that $n_j \in \text{Dom}(H)$ for each $j \in [\nu_i]$.
			We know that $H|_B = h_B$.
			Now suppose $f_{i'}(\bv{n}') \in \text{Dom}(H)$ for some $i' \in I$ and $\bv{n}' \in N^{\nu_{i'}}$.
			This means there is some $j' \in [\nu_i]$ such that $f_{i'}(\bv{n}') \in \text{Dom}(H_{j'})$.
			But since $H_{j'} \in \mc{H}$ this means $n'_j\in \text{Dom}(H_{j'})$ for each $j \in [\nu_{i'}]$ which means these same elements are also in $H$.
			Because $f_{i'}(\bv{n}') \in H_{j'}$, we have that $H_{j'}(f_{i'}(\bv{n}')) = h_{f_i}(H_{j'}^{(\nu_{i'})}(\bv{n}'))$.
			But this also means $H(f_{i'}(\bv{n}')) = h_{f_i}(H^{(\nu_{i'})}(\bv{n}'))$.
			All of this means $H \in \mc{H}$.
			But, now, consider
			\begin{equation*}
				H' = H \cup \{\braket{f_i(\bv{n}), h_{f_i}(H^{(\nu_i)}(\bv{n}))}\}
			\end{equation*}
			By unique readability we know $f_i(\bv{n}) \not \in B$ so $H'|_B = h_B$ still.
			We also know that there is no other $j \in I$ such that $f_i(\bv{n}) \in \text{Ran}(f_{j})$ and there is no other $\bv{n}' \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ such that $f_i(\bv{n}) = f_i(\bv{n}')$.
			This means that the conditions on $f_i(\bv{n})$ within $H \in \mc{H}$ are satisfied.
			This means $H' \in \mc{H}$.
			But this also means that $f_i(\bv{n}) \in \text{Dom}(h)$.
			
			We know that each $H\in \mc{H}$ has $H|_B = h_B$ so we know $h|_B = h_B$.
			We also know that each $H \in \mc{H}$ satisfies the recursive condition on $\mc{H}$.
			But this means $h$ also satisfies that condition which means $h \in \mc{H}$ and that for each $i \in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ that
			\begin{equation*}
				h(f_i(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(h^{(\nu_i)}(\bv{n})).
			\end{equation*}
			So we see that $h$ satisfies both recursion conditions.
			
			Now suppose $h': N \to S$ satisifes $h'|_B = h_B$ and the recursive condition.
			Then $h'\in \mc{H}$ which means $h = h'$ since $h, h' \in \mc{H}$ and they have the same domain, namely $N$.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		Our first use of the recursion theorem will be to define the set of predecessors of an element $n\in N$.
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Predecessor Set]
			Suppose $\bv{f} = (f_i)_{i \in I}$ is a $\bv{\nu}$-ary $N$ function family and $N$ is inductively generated and exhibits unique readability.
			We will use the recursion theorem to define a function 
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Pred}: N \to \mc{P}(N)
			\end{equation*}
			which satisifes
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{$\text{Pred}(b) = \emptyset$ if $b \in B$}
				\item{for all $i \in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$, $\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n})) = \bigcup_{j\in [\nu_i]} (\text{Pred}(n_j) \cup \{n_j\})$}
			\end{itemize}
			
			We will proceed in a few stages.
			
			For the set $S$ in the recursion theorem we take $S = N \times \mathcal{P}(N)$.
			For $h_B$ we define $h_B(b) = (b, \emptyset)$ for each $b\in B$.
			Now consider $i \in I$ and the corresponding $f_i$ and $h_{f_i}$.
			We must define $h_{f_i}(\bv{s})$ for $\bv{s} \in S^{\nu_i}$.
			Choose $\bv{n} \in N^{\nu_i}$ such that $n_i = (s_i)_1$ and let $P_i = (s_i)_2$.
			We then define $h_f$ by
			\begin{equation*}
				h_{f_i}(\bv{s}) = \left(f_i(\bv{n}), \bigcup_{i=1}^{n_f} \left(P_i \cup \{n_i\}\right)\right)
			\end{equation*}
			
			The structural recursion theorem then asserts the existence of a function $h: N\to N \times \mathcal{P}(N)$ such that
			
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{$h(b) = (b, \emptyset)$ for $b\in B$}
				\item{$h(f(\bv{n})) = h_f(h^{(n_f)}(\bv{n})) $.}
			\end{itemize}
			
			We will prove, by induction, that $(h(n))_1 = n$.
			If $n\in b$ then $(h(n))_1 = (b, \emptyset)_1 = b$ as needed.
			Now consider $i\in I$, and $\bv{n}\in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ and assume that for each $j\in [\nu_i]$ that $(h(n_j))_1 = n_j$.
			Let 
			\begin{equation*}
				s_j = h(n_j) = ((h(n_j))_1, (h(n_j))_2) = (n_j, (h(n_j))_2).
			\end{equation*}
			where we've used the induction hypothesis that $(h(n_j))_1 = n_j$.
			Note that $\bv{s} = h^{(\nu_i)}(\bv{n})$
			But then
			\begin{equation*}
				h(f_i(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(h^{(\nu_i)}(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(\bv{s}) = \left(f(\bv{n}), \bigcup_{j\in [\nu_i]} ((h(n_j))_2 \cup \{n_j\})\right).
			\end{equation*}
			So we can see that $(h(f_i(\bv{n})))_1 = f(\bv{n})$.
			This concludes the proof by induction that $(h(n))_1 = n$.
			
			But, consider now $\text{Pred}: N \to \mc{P}(N)$ defined by
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Pred}: N \to& \mc{P}(N)\\
				n \mapsto& (h(n))_2
			\end{align*}
			We can see from the equation above that for each $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ that 
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n})) = \bigcup_{j\in [\nu_i]} (\text{Pred}(n_j) \cup \{n_j\})
			\end{align*}
			and of course we have that $\text{Pred}(b) = (h(b))_2 = (b, \emptyset)_2 = \emptyset$.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		The induction principle allows us to prove a principle $\phi(n)$ for all $n\in N$ by 
		\begin{itemize}
			\item{proving $\phi(b)$ for all $b\in B$}
			\item{proving for each $i\in I$ and $\bv{n}\in\text{Dom}(f_i) \subset  N^{\nu_i}$ that if we assume $\phi(n_j)$ for each $j\in [\nu_i]$ we can prove $\phi(f_i(\bv{n}))$.}
		\end{itemize}
		However, now that we have defined $\text{Pred}$, it is possible to formulate a strong induction principle which allows us to assume a stronger hypothesis during the induction step.
		This stronger hypothesis is to assume $\phi(n')$ for each $n'\in \text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))$.
		This additional information may make it possible to prove $\phi(f_i(\bv{n}))$ when it otherwise may not have been possible.
		We now state and prove the strong induction principle.
		When necessary, we will call the previously proven induction principle the \textit{weak} induction principle.
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Strong Induction Principle]
			Suppose $\bv{f} = (f_i)_{i\in I}$ is a $\bv{\nu}$-ary $N$ function family and $N$ is $(B, \bv{f})$ inductively generated.
			Suppose also that $N$ exhibits unique readability so that $\text{Pred}$ is defined.
			Consider an expression $\phi(n)$ which depends on $n\in N$.

			Now consider the set
			\begin{equation*}
				\bar{N} = \{n\in N \mid \phi(n)\}
			\end{equation*}
			
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{Base Case: Suppose we can prove $\phi(n)$ holds for each $n\in B$ so that $B\subset \bar{N}$.}
				\item{(Strong) Inductive Step: Now consider $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ and $f_i(\bv{n})$. Suppose that if we assume $\phi(n')$ holds for each $n' \in \text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))$ that we can prove $\phi(f(\bv{n}))$ holds.}
			\end{itemize}
			
			We will use the weak induction principle to prove this.
			So we assume that we can prove everything in the strong induction base case and strong inductive step.
			We now define the set
			\begin{equation*}
				\bar{N}' = \{n\in N \mid \phi(n) \text{ and for all } n'\in \text{Pred}(n), \phi(n')\} 
			\end{equation*}
			We will prove that $\bar{N}'$ is inductive.
			We have already proven $B\subset \bar{N}$ for the strong induction base case.
			Since $\text{Pred}(n) = \emptyset$ for $n\in B$ this means that $B\subset \bar{N}'$ as well.
			Now consider $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ and suppose that $n_j \in \bar{N}'$ for each $j \in [\nu_i]$.
			In particular, this means that $\phi(n_j)$ holds and $\phi(n'_j)$ for each $j\in [\nu_i]$ and for each $n'_j \in \text{Pred}(n_j)$.
			But
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n})) = \bigcup_{j\in [\nu_i]} (\{n_j\} \cup \text{Pred}(n_j)).
			\end{equation*}
			Based on this definition, we can see that $\phi(n')$ holds for each $n' \in \text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))$.
			But then the strong induction step tells us that this means $\phi(f_i(\bv{n}))$ holds so that $f_i(\bv{n}) \in \bar{N}'$ and, therefore, that $\bar{N}'$ is inductive.
			
			Since $\bar{N}'$ is an inductive subset of an inductively generated set, we have $\bar{N}' = N$.
			But, because $\bar{N}'$ is based on a stronger condition that $\bar{N}$ we have that $\bar{N}' \subset \bar{N}$ so
			\begin{equation*}
				N = \bar{N}' \subset \bar{N} \subset N
			\end{equation*}
			which means $\bar{N} = N$.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		The structural recursion theorem allows us to take $\bv{n}$ and $h(n_i)$ for each $i\in [n]$ to construct $h(f_i(\bv{n}))$.
		But what if we require knowledge of the value of $h$ on earlier predecessors of $f_i(\bv{n})$, $\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))$, to determine the next value of $h$? 
		The strong structural recursion theorem addresses this.
		When referring to the previous recursion theorem we may call it the weak structural recursion theorem to distinguish it from the forthcoming strong structural recursion theorem.
		The proof of the strong structural recursion theorem will make use of the strong induction principle.
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Strong Structural Recursion]
			Suppose $\bv{f} = (f_i)_{i\in I}$ is a $\bv{\nu}$-ary $N$ function family and $N$ is inductively generated and exhibits unique readability.
			Suppose that there is a set $S$ and a function $h_B: B \to S$.
			
			An $S$-history for $n\in N$ is a function $g:\text{Pred}(n) \to S$.
			We define
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Hist}_S^n = \{g \in S^N \mid g: \text{Pred}(n) \to S\}
			\end{align*}
			and
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Hist}_S = \bigcup_{n\in N} \text{Hist}_S^n.
			\end{align*}
			For each $i\in I$, we also define
			\begin{align*}
				D_{f_i} = \{(\bv{n}, g) \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \times \text{Hist}_S: g \in \text{Hist}_S^{f_i(\bv{n})} \}
			\end{align*}
			We then suppose now, that for each $i\in I$ there is a function $h_{f_i}: D_{f_i} \to S$.
			
			Under these conditions, the strong structural recursion theorem then gives us the unique existence of a function $h: N \to S$ such that
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{$h|_B = h_B$}
				\item{$h(f_i(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(\bv{n}, h|_{\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))})$ For each $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$}
			\end{itemize}
			We can see that we have defined $D_{f_i}$ and $h_{f_i}$ as we have so that the inductive step (via $h_{f_i}$) has access both to $\bv{n}$, the elements we are recursing on, and the value of $h$ on these elements and their predecessors, i.e. $h|_{\text{Pred}(f(\bv{n}))}$.
			
			We will prove this in a very similar fashion to the proof of the weak recursion principle.
			
			We define
			\begin{align*}
				\mc{H} = \{& H \in \mc{P}(N\times S) \mid H \text{ is functional and}\\
				&H|_B = h_B \text{ and }\\
				&\text{for all } i \in I \text{ and } \bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i},\\
				&\text{that if } f_i(\bv{n}) \in \text{Dom}(H) \text{ then}\\
				&\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n})) \subset \text{Dom}(H) \text{ and}\\
				&H(f_i(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(\bv{n}, H|_{\text{Pred}(f(\bv{n}))})\}
			\end{align*}
			
			We must prove that $h$ is total on $N$, functional, satisfies the desired properties, and is unique.
			
			We first prove that if $H_1, H_2 \in H$ then $H_1$ and $H_2$ agree on their common domain $D = \text{Dom}(H_1) \cap \text{Dom}(H_2)$.
			We prove this by strong induction on $N$.
			Specifically, we prove that if $n\in D$ then $H_1(n) = H_2(n)$.
			For the base case, if $n\in B$, we know that $H_1(n) = H_2(n) = h_B(n)$.
			For the strong induction case we consider $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$.
			We assume that for each $n' \in \text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))$ that $H_1(n') = H_2(n')$.
			If $f_i(\bv{n}) \not \in D$ then there is nothing to prove.
			If $f_i(\bv{n}) \in D$ then we know $n'\in D$ for each $n' \in \text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))$.
			But we know that $H_1$ and $H_2$ agree on each $n'$.
			But we also know that
			\begin{equation*}
				H_1(f_i(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(\bv{n}, H_1|_{\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))}) = h_{f_i}(\bv{n}, H_2|_{\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))}) = H_2(f_i(\bv{n}))
			\end{equation*}
			where the middle equality follows because $H_1$ and $H_2$ agree on $\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))$, and the outer equalities follow from the condition that $H_1, H_2 \in \mc{H}$.
			But, since all pairs $H_1, H_2 \in \mc{H}$ agree on their common domains, this means $h \in \bigcup \mc{H}$ is functional.
			
			We prove by strong induction that $h$ is total.
			We know that $H|_B = h_B$ for each $H\in B$ so this means $h|_B = h_B$.
			Now consider $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ and assume $n' \in \text{Dom}(h)$ for each $n' \in \text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))$.
			We must show $f_i(\bv{n}) \in \text{Dom}(h)$.
			Because $n' \in \text{Dom}(H)$, this means there is an $H_{n'} \in \mc{H}$ with $n' \in \text{Dom}(H_{n'})$.
			Consider the set
			\begin{equation*}
				H = \bigcup_{n' \in \text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))} H_{n'}.
			\end{equation*}
			We have that $n' \in \text{Dom}(H)$ for each $n' \in \text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))$.
			We know $H|_B = h_B$.
			Now suppose $f_{i'}(\bv{n}'') \in \text{Dom}(H)$ for some $i' \in I$ and $\bv{n}'' \in N^{\nu_{i'}}$.
			This means there is some $n'$ such that $f_{i'}(\bv{n}') \in \text{Dom}(H_{n'})$.
			But since $H_{n'} \in \mc{H}$, this means $n'' \in \text{Dom}(H)_{n'}$ for each $n'' \in \text{Pred}(f_{i'}(\bv{n}''))$ which means these same elements are also in $H$.
			Because $f_{i'}(\bv{n}'') \in H_{n'}$, we have that $H_{n'}(\bv{n}'', f_{i'}(\bv{n}'')) = h_{f_{i'}}(H_{n'}|_{\text{Pred}(f_{i'}(\bv{n}''))})$.
			But this also means $H(f_{i'}(\bv{n}'')) = h_{f_{i'}}(\bv{n}'', H|_{\text{Pred}(f_{i'}(\bv{n}''))})$.
			All of this means $H \in \mc{H}$.
			But, now consider
			\begin{equation*}
				H' = H \cup \braket{f_i(\bv{n}), h_{f_i}(\bv{n}, H|_{\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))}}.
			\end{equation*}
			By unique readability, we know $f_i(\bv{n}) \not \in B$ so $H'|_B = h_B$ still.
			We also know there is no other $j\in I$ such that $f_i(\bv{n}) \in \text{Ran}(f_j)$ and there is no other $\bv{n}' \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ such that $f_i(\bv{n}) = f_{i'}(\bv{n})$.
			This means that the conditions on $f_i(\bv{n})$ within $H \in \mc{H}$ are satisfied.
			This means $H' \in \mc{H}$.
			But this also means $f_i(\bv{n}) \in \text{Dom}(h)$ concluding our proof that $h$ is total on $N$.
			
			We know that each $H \in \mc{H}$ has $H|_B = h_B$ we so know $h|_B = h_B$.
			We also know that for each $H\in \mc{H}$ the recursive condition on $\mc{H}$ holds.
			But this means $h$ also satisfies that condition which means $h\in \mc{H}$ and that for each $i\in I$ and $\bv{n} \in \text{Dom}(f_i) \subset N^{\nu_i}$ that
			\begin{equation*}
				h(f_i(\bv{n})) = h_{f_i}(\bv{n}, H|_{\text{Pred}(f_i(\bv{n}))}).
			\end{equation*}
			So we see that $h$ satisfies both recursion conditions.
			
			Now suppose $h': N \to S$ satisfies $h'|_B = h_B$ and the recursive condition.
			Then $h' \in \mc{H}$ which means $h = h'$ since $h, h' \in \mc{H}$ and they have the same domain, namely $N$.
		\end{preformal theorem}
	
		As a first application of the strong structural recursion theorem will prove that, for a binary operation $\ast: A \times A \to A$, that binary associativity of $\ast$ leads to general associativity of $\ast$.
		Binary associativity means that, for all $a_1, a_2, a_3 \in A$, that 
		\begin{equation*}
			(a_1 \ast a_2) \ast a_3 = a_1 \ast (a_2 \ast a_3).
		\end{equation*}
		Intuitively, generalized associativity of $\ast$ means that if we concatenate an arbitrary number of elements of $A$ using $\ast$, then we get the same result regardless of where we place the parentheses.
		For example, consider $a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4 \in A$.
		There are five possible parenthesizations of these 4 elements:
		\begin{align*}
			&a_1 \ast (a_2 \ast (a_3 \ast a_4))\\
			&a_1 \ast ((a_2 \ast a_3) \ast a_4)\\
			&(a_1 \ast a_2) \ast (a_3 \ast a_4)\\
			&(a_1 \ast (a_2 \ast a_3)) \ast a_4\\
			&((a_1 \ast a_2) \ast a_3) \ast a_4.
		\end{align*}
		If $\ast$ exhibited generalized associativity then all 5 of these expressions would be equal.
		The statement of general associativity of $\ast$ will be that for any $n \in \N$ and any $n$-tuple $\bv{a} \in A^n$, that all parenthesization of $\bv{a}$ are equal.
		However, we have yet to provide a rigorous definition of a parenthesization of $\bv{a}$.
		In this next section we will give a definition for parenthesizations and prove that binary associativity implies generalized associativity for any binary operation.
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Tuple Slicing]
			Suppose $\bv{a} \in A^n$ is an $n$-tuple for some $n\in \N$.
			if $1 \le i, j \le n$ then we define $\bv{a}_{i:j}$ to be the $j-1+1$ tuple satisfying
			\begin{equation*}
				\bv{a}_{i:j}(k) = a_{k+i-1}
			\end{equation*}
			where $1 \le k \le j-i+1$.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		Suppose $\ast: A\times A \to A$ is a binary operation.
		We seek a function $\text{Par}: A^* \to \mc{P}(A)$ which satisfies
		\begin{itemize}
			\item{$\text{Par}(\varnothing) = \emptyset$}
			\item{$\text{Par}(a) = \{a\}$ where $\text{Par}(a)$ is notating $\text{Par}((a))$ with $a\in A$ and $(a) \in A^1$.}
			\item{$\text{Par}(\bv{a}) = \bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1} \{x \ast y \in A: x\in \text{Par}(\bv{a}_{1:k}) \text{ and } y \in \text{Par}(\bv{a}_{k+1:n})\}$ for $\bv{a} \in A^n$ with $n>1$}
		\end{itemize}		
		Since the definition of $\text{Par}$ depends on itself, we see that $\text{Par}$ must be defined using the recursion.
		
		We will define $\text{Par}$ using the strong recursion principle applied to $\N^+ = \N \setminus \{0\}$.
		We note that this set is $(\{1\}, \{+_1\})$ inductively generated where $+_1: \N^+ \to \N^+$ is the function that takes $n \to n+1$.
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Parenthesizations]
			We let
			\begin{equation*}
				S = \{(n, f) \in \N^+\times \mc{P}(A)^{A^{>0}} \mid f: A^n \to \mc{P}(A)\}
			\end{equation*}
			The idea is that $f$ will be the parenthesization function specialised to domain $A^n$ and $n$ is carried along as a tag needed for technical reasons during th recursion construction.
			
			Let $h_B(1) = (1, f_1)$ where $f_1(a) = \{a\}$.
			Now consider $h_{+_1}: D_{+_1} \to S$.
			In this case
			\begin{equation*}
				D_{+1} = \{(n, g) \in \N^+ \times \text{Hist}_S: g \in \text{Hist}_S^{n+1}\}.
			\end{equation*}
			Note that we have identified $(\N^+)^1$ with $\N^+$ under the association $(n) \to n$.
			We let
			\begin{equation*}
				h_{+_1}(n, g) = (n, f_{n, g})
			\end{equation*}
			with $g \in \text{Hist}_S^{n+1}$.
			We define $f_{n, g}: A^n \to \mc{P}(A)$ by
			\begin{equation*}
				f_{n, g}(\bv{a}) = \bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1} \{x\ast y \in A \mid x\in g_2(k)(\bv{a}_{1:k}) \text{ and } y \in g_2(n-k)(\bv{a}_{k+1:n})\}.
			\end{equation*}
			Note that $g(k)\in S$ so it is a tuple $(n, f)$ with $f:A^n \to \mc{P}(A)$.
			$g_2(k)$ picks out the second component of this tuple, $f$.
			
			The strong structural recursion theorem then gives us the unique existence of a function $h: \N^+ \to S$ which satisfies
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{$h(1) = h_B(1) = (1, f_1)$ with $f_1((a)) = \{a\}$}
				\item{$h(n+1) = h_{+_1}(n, h|_{[n]})$, noting that $\text{Pred}(n+1) = [n]$.}
			\end{itemize}
			We have that 
			\begin{equation*}
				h_{+_1}(n, h|_{[n]}) = (n, f_{n, h|_{[n]}})
			\end{equation*}
			with, for $\bv{a} \in A^n$
			\begin{equation*}
				f_{n, h|_{[n]}}(\bv{a}) = \bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1} \{x\ast y\in A \mid x\in h_2(k)(\bv{a}_{1:k})\text{ and } y \in h_2(k)(\bv{a}_{k+1:n})\}.
			\end{equation*}
			
			We now define 
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Par}: A^*\to& \mc{P}(A)\\
				\bv{a} \mapsto& \begin{cases} \emptyset &\text{ if } \text{Len}(\bv{a}) = 0\\
					h_2(\text{Len}(\bv{a}))(\bv{a}) &\text{ if } \text{Len}(\bv{a}) > 0
					\end{cases}
			\end{align*}

			Unravelling the definitions, we can see
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{$\text{Par}(()) = \emptyset$}
				\item{$\text{Par}((a)) = \{a\}$}
				\item{$\text{Par}(\bv{a}) = \bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1}\{x\ast y \in A \mid x \in \text{Par}(\bv{a}_{1:k}) \text{ and } y \in \text{Par}(\bv{a}_{k+1:n})\}$}
			\end{itemize}
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		With this definition in hand, we can, then, define generalized associativity to mean that for any $\bv{a} \in A^*$ that $x, y \in \text{Par}(\bv{a})$ implies $x=y$.

		\begin{preformal theorem}[Binary Associativity Implies Generalized Associativity]
			Suppose $\ast: A \times A \to A$ exhibits binary associativity.
			We will prove that $\ast$ also exhibits generalized associativity which means that for any $\bv{a} \in A^*$ the if $x, y \in \text{Par}(\bv{a})$ then $x=y$.
			
			If $\text{Len}(\bv{a}) = 0$ then $\text{Par}(\bv{a}) = \emptyset$ so there are no $x, y \in \text{Par}(\bv{a})$ to check.
			
			For $\text{Len}(\bv{a}) \ge 1$ we will proceed by strong induction on $\N^+$.
			Our statement $\phi(n)$ is the statement that for all $\bv{a} \in A^n$ that $x, y \in \text{Par}(\bv{a})$ implies $x=y$. 
			There is, then, one unique element of $\text{Par}(\bv{a})$ which we denote by $\ast(\bv{a})$.
			The base case is immediate because for $(a) \in A^1$, $\text{Par}((a)) = \{a\}$, a singleton.
			
			Now suppose that $\phi(n)$ holds for all $n\in \text{Pred}(n+1) = [n]$. 
			We must show that $\phi(n+1)$ holds.
			Let $\bv{a} \in A^{n+1}$ and consider
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Par}(\bv{a}) = \bigcup_{k=1}^{n+1-1} \{x\ast y \in A \mid x\in \text{Par}(\bv{a}_{1:k}) \text{ and } y\in \text{Par}(\bv{a}_{k+1:n+1})\}
			\end{align*}
			However, by the strong induction hypothesis, there only one unique element in each of $\text{Par}(\bv{a}_{1:k})$ and $\text{Par}(\bv{a}_{k+1:n+1})$ because $\bv{a}_{1:k}$ and $\bv{a}_{k+1:n+1}$ are both tuples with length  $\le n$.
			We can then write
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Par}(\bv{a}) =& \bigcup_{k=1}^n \{x \ast y \in A \mid x = \ast(\bv{a}_{1:k}) \text{ and } y = \ast(\bv{a}_{k+1:n+1})\}\\
				=&  \{\ast(\bv{a}_{1:k}) \ast \ast(\bv{a}_{k+1:n+1}) \in A \mid 1 \le k \le n\}
			\end{align*}
			But now choose $i, j$ with $1 \le i < j \le n+1$ and consider
			\begin{align*}
				\ast(\bv{a}_{1:i}) \ast \ast(\bv{a}_{i+1:n+1}) =& \ast(\bv{a}_{1:i}) \ast (\ast(\bv{a}_{i+1:j}) \ast \ast(\bv{a}_{j+1:n+1}))\\
				=& (\ast(\bv{a}_{1:i}) \ast \ast(\bv{a}_{i+1:j})) \ast \ast(\bv{a}_{j+1:n+1})\\
				=& \ast(\bv{a}_{1:j}) \ast \ast(\bv{a}_{j+1:n+1}) 
			\end{align*}
			where the first and third equalities follow by considering different parenthesizations of tuples with length $\le n$ and the second equality follows by binary associativity.
			But this means that all of the terms in the set above for $\text{Par}(\bv{a})$ are equal to eachother.
			We denote this unique element by $\ast(\bv{a})$.
			
			This concludes our proof that binary associativity implies generalized associativity.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\section{List Operations}
			\begin{preformal definition}[List Concatenation]
				We define $\ast: S^* \times S^* \to S^*$ by
				\begin{align*}
					\ast(\bv{s}, \bv{t}) = \bv{s} \ast \bv{t}
				\end{align*}
				with
				\begin{align*}
					\norm{\bv{s} \ast \bv{t}} = \norm{\bv{s}} + \norm{\bv{t}}
				\end{align*}
				and for $1 \le i \le \norm{\bv{s}\cdot \bv{t}}$
				\begin{align*}
					(\bv{s}\ast \bv{t})_i = \begin{cases}
						s_i &\text{ if } i \le \norm{\bv{s}}\\
						t_{i - \norm{\bv{s}}} &\text{ if } \norm{\bv{s}} + 1 \le i \le \norm{\bv{s}\cdot \bv{t}}.
					\end{cases}
				\end{align*}
				If $\bv{s} = \bv{t} = ()$ then $\bv{s} \ast \bv{t} = ()$.
				
				We will use juxtaposition to indicate concatenation.
				That is,
				\begin{equation*}
					\bv{s}\bv{t} = \bv{s} \ast \bv{t}
				\end{equation*}
			\end{preformal definition}
	
			\begin{preformal theorem}[List Concatenation is Binary Associative]
				Suppose $\bv{a}, \bv{b}, \bv{c} \in S^*$
				We wish to show
				\begin{equation*}
					\bv{a}(\bv{b}\bv{c}) = (\bv{a}\bv{b})\bv{c}
				\end{equation*}
				
				We have that
				\begin{align*}
					\norm{\bv{a}(\bv{b}\bv{c})} = \norm{\bv{a}} + \norm{\bv{b}\bv{c}} = \norm{\bv{a}} + \norm{\bv{b}} + \norm{\bv{c}} = \norm{\bv{a}\bv{b}} + \norm{\bv{c}} = \norm{\bv{a}\bv{b}\bv{c}}.
				\end{align*}
				Note that this follows by binary associativity of addition on $\N$ which we assume as granted as a preformal concept.
				
				We will show that $(\bv{a}(\bv{b}\bv{c}))_i = ((\bv{a}\bv{b})\bv{c})_i$ for all $i$ with $1 \le i \le \norm{\bv{a}} + \norm{\bv{b}} + \norm{\bv{c}}$.
		
				First suppose $1 \le i \le \norm{\bv{a}}$.
				Then 
				\begin{equation*}
					(\bv{a}(\bv{b}\bv{c}))_i = a_i
				\end{equation*}
				but 
				\begin{equation*}
					((\bv{a}\bv{b})\bv{c})_i = (\bv{a}\bv{b})_i = a_i
				\end{equation*}
				
				Now suppose $\norm{\bv{a}} + 1 \le i \le \norm{\bv{a}} + \norm{\bv{b}}$.
				Then 
				\begin{equation*}
					(\bv{a}(\bv{b}\bv{c})) = (\bv{b}\bv{c})_{i-\norm{\bv{a}}} = b_{i-\norm{\bv{a}}}
				\end{equation*}
				but
				\begin{equation*}
					((\bv{a}\bv{b})\bv{c})_i = (\bv{a}\bv{b})_i = b_{i-\norm{\bv{a}}}
				\end{equation*}
				
				Now suppose $\norm{\bv{a}} + \norm{\bv{b}}+1 \le i \le \norm{\bv{a}} + \norm{\bv{b}} + \norm{\bv{c}}$.
				Then
				\begin{equation*}
					(\bv{a}(\bv{b}\bv{c}))_i = (\bv{b}\bv{c})_{i-\norm{\bv{a}}} = c_{i-\norm{\bv{a}} - \norm{\bv{b}}}
				\end{equation*}
				but
				\begin{equation*}
					((\bv{a}\bv{b})\bv{c})_i = c_{i-\norm{\bv{a}\bv{c}}} = c_{i-\norm{\bv{a}} - \norm{\bv{b}}}.
				\end{equation*}
				
				Note if $\bv{a}, \bv{b}, \bv{c} = ()$ then $\bv{a}(\bv{b}\bv{c}) = (\bv{a}(\bv{b}\bv{c})) = ()$.
				
				This all proves that $\bv{a}(\bv{b}\bv{c}) = (\bv{a}\bv{b})\bv{c}$.
			\end{preformal theorem}
	
			\begin{preformal theorem}[List Concatenation Exhibits Generalized Associativity]
				We proved above that if any binary operation exhibits binary associativity then it exhibits generalized associativity.
				Since list concatenation exhibits binary associativity it must exhibit generalized associativity.
				
				Suppose $\bv{\alpha} \in (A^*)^*$.
				We have $\text{Par}(\bv{\alpha})$ is the set of all parenthesizations on the entries of $\bv{\alpha}$, but, because of generalized associativity, we know that $\text{Par}(\bv{\alpha})$ is a singleton which we will denote $\circledast(\bv{\alpha})$.
				We examine the following operations
				\begin{itemize}
					\item{$\ast: A^* \times A^* \to A^*$, this is the standard binary concatenation operation defined above.}
					\item{$\circledast: (A^*)^* \to A^*$, this is the generalized concatenation oepration which takes a list of lists and returns a list that is the result of concatenating all the input lists}
					\item{$\circ: (A^*)^* \times (A^*)^* \to (A^*)^*$ this is standard list concatenation but acting on lists of lists}
					\item{$\circledcirc: ((A^*)^*)^* \to (A^*)^*$ this operation takes a list of lists of lists and returns a list of lists. We only use it here to define an overloaded concatenation operation which allows us to concatenate objects from multiple layers of this heirarchy.}
				\end{itemize}
				
				Let $D = (A^*)^* \cup A^* \cup \Sigma$.
				We define a function $\text{Normalize}: D^* \to ((A^*)^*)^*$
				\begin{align*}
					\text{Normalize}(\bv{d})_i = \begin{cases}
						d_i &\text{ if } d_i \in (A^*)^*\\
						(d_i) & \text{ if } d_i \in A^*\\
						((d_i)) & \text{ if } d_i \in A
					\end{cases}
				\end{align*}
				for each $i\in [\norm{\bv{d}}]$.
				We then define an overloaded concatenation operation which allows us to concatenate arbitary elements whether they are in $A$, $A^*$, or $(A^*)^*$.
				We define $\text{Cat}: D^* \to A^*$ by 
				\begin{equation*}
					\text{Cat}(\bv{d}) = \circledast(\circledcirc(\text{Normalize}(\bv{d})))
				\end{equation*}
				
				So, for example, if $a\in A$, $\bv{a} \in A^*$ and $\bv{\alpha}\in (A^*)^*$, then
				\begin{equation*}
					\text{Cat}(a, \bv{a}, \bv{\alpha}) = \circledast(\circledcirc(((a)), (\bv{a}), \bv{\alpha})) = a \bv{a}\bv{\alpha} 
				\end{equation*} 
				We use juxtaposiiton as a shorthand for the overloaded $\text{Cat}$ operation.
				Note that when an element $\bv{\alpha} \in (A^*)^*$ stands alone in an expression it may be ambiguous whether we are referring to e.g. $\bv{\alpha}\in (A^*)^*$ or $\text{Cat}(\bv{\alpha}) \in A^*$.
				Likewise for an element $a\in A$.
				In such ambiguous cases we will explicitly use the $\text{Cat}$ notation to disambiguate.
				
			\end{preformal theorem}
	
	\chapter{Language of First Order Logic}
	
		We now have the required preformal concepts to begin developing  \gls{lfol}.
		\gls{lfol} is a formal language which, here, means a set of symbols and syntax rules that allows us to form those symbols into valid strings of symbols.
		We will begin by specifying the \text{alphabet} for our language, that is, the set of symbols which we will use to construct everything in our language.
		The language is contained in the set of symbols $\Sigma$.
		
		Next, we will discuss how variables, constants, and function symbols can be combined to form terms within the language.
		Semantically speaking, terms are the \textit{subjects} of our language. 
		That is, terms are the things about which the language makes statements.
		Terms are captured in $\textbf{Term} \subset \Sigma^*$.	
		$\textbf{Term}$ is defined as an inductive closure with a certain base set and formation rules.
		
		Then we discuss how terms can be inserted into predicates to form atomic formulas.
		Semantically, a predicate is a true or false statement about one or more objects (terms).
		We then discuss how logical connectives can be used to combine atomic formulas into more complicated logical expressions.
		The object we have at the end of this construction is $\textbf{PForm}$, the set of permissive formulas.
		Again, $\textbf{PForm}$ is constructed as an inductive closure.
		
		$\textbf{PForm}$ stands for the set of \textit{permissive} formulas.
		This is because it permits two sorts of degenerate quantification.
		Here we restrict to $\textbf{SForm}$, the set of strict formulas which don't exhibit these degenericies.
		As far as I can tell this is a non-standard move for logical treatments. 
		We make the choice to reject these degeneracies so that our subsequent proof better reflect typical mathematical reasoning.
		
		Along the way towards developing $\textbf{SForm}$ we define numerous recursively functions required to parse, operate on, and understand (pre)formally the formulas of the language.
		We also develop techincal results such as the unique readability of $\textbf{Term}$ and $\textbf{PForm}$.
		
		Loosely speaking, what we here call $\textbf{PForm}$ is most closely related to what other texts, such as Forbes, call \gls{lfol}.
		After defining $\textbf{PForm}$ and $\textbf{SForm}$ we will have use for both going forward. 
		For example, sometimes we will use the more general $\textbf{PForm}$ structure to define functions which restrict to $\textbf{SForm}$. 
		So it is not clear that there is one thing in this text which we should call \gls{lfol}.
		Instead, we will target $\textbf{PForm}$ and use language around these sets going forward.
		
		Finally, though we made reference to semantic interpretations of elements of the langauge above, we remind the reader that this is purely to help the reader form an intuitive and understanding and is not required at all for the syntactic development of the framework.
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[The Alphabet of $\textbf{PForm}$]
			We will use the following symbols in $\textbf{PForm}$:
			\begin{equation*}
				\textbf{Symb} = \{v, c, P, f, \implies, \lnot, \forall\}
			\end{equation*}
			$v$ will be used for variables, $c$ for constants, $P$ for predicates, $f$ for functions.
			$\implies$, $\lnot$, and $\forall$ are logical symbols.
			
			From these we define the following sets
			\begin{align*}
				\textbf{Var} =& ((v, n))_{n\in \N} = (v_n)_{n\in \N}\\
				\textbf{Const} =& ((c, n))_{n\in \N} = (c_n)_{n\in \N}\\				
				\textbf{Pred} =& ((P, \nu, n))_{(\nu, n) \in \N \times \N} = (P_n^{(\nu)})_{(\nu, n) \in \N\times \N}\\
				\textbf{Func} =& ((f, \nu, n))_{(\nu, n) \in \N^+ \times \N} = (f_n^{(\nu)})_{(\nu, n) \in \N^+ \times \N}\\				
				\textbf{Log} =& \{\implies, \lnot\}\\
				\textbf{Quant} =& \{\forall\}
			\end{align*}
			Here $n$ is to be thought of as a subscript to distinguish e.g. one constant from another.
			$\nu$ indicates the \qq{arity} of a function or predicate.
			Note that we allow $0$-ary predicates which, semantically, are like expressions that simply evaluate to a truth value with no input.
			However, we do not allow $0$-ary functions since these would be redundant with constants.
			Because constants are treated differently than functions in the various inductive structures we choose separate them out into their own category rather than lumping them in with function as $0$-ary functions.
			
			The alphabet of $\text{PForm}$ is the union of all of these
			\begin{equation*}
				\Sigma = \bigcup \{\textbf{Var}, \textbf{Const}, \textbf{Pred}, \textbf{Func}, \textbf{Log}, \textbf{Quant}\}
			\end{equation*}
			If $\sigma \in \Sigma^*$ is a $\Sigma$ list we say $\sigma$ is a \gls{lfol} string.
			

		\end{preformal definition}
		
		We will highlight that now, for the first time, we are beginning to define objects of our \textit{formal} language.
		This is in contrast to all of the work thus far which has been preformal.
		When we are discussing concepts within the formal language \textit{using} the preformal framework, we will frequently uses aliases for symbols or strings from the formal language.
		For example, we will use letters from near the end of the alphabet like $x$, $y$, $z$ to denote variables however, these should be understood to be aliases for true symbols of the formal language such as $v_1$, $v_2$, $v_3$.
		Similarly we will work with predicates like $\in$, $\subset$, and $=$ which may be aliases for, e.g. $P^{(2)}_0$, $P^{(2)}_1$, and $P^{(2)}_2$ and functions like $\bigcup$ and $\mc{P}$ which may be aliases for, e.g. $f^{(1)}_0$ and $f^{(1)}_1$.
		Notably, the symbol $\curlywedge$ will be used as a $0$-ary predicate and it will have a special role in our natural deduction system.
		We will consider $\curlywedge$ to be an alias for $P_0^{(0)}$.
		We denote \qq{is an alias for} using $\equiv$ so that
		\begin{equation*}
			\curlywedge \equiv P_0^{(0)}
		\end{equation*}
		
		We have only admitted $\implies$ and $\lnot$ as logical operators and $\forall$ as a quantifier into our language. 
		Notably, we have left out $\land$, $\lor$, $\iff$ and $\exists$.
		This has been done in an effort to keep the formal language as concise as possible so as to reduce the amount of work needed for the various metalogical proof. 
		We will introduce these other symbols later as aliases or abbreviations of expressions in terms of symbols that are included in \gls{lfol}:
		\begin{align*}
			\lor PQ &\equiv \implies \lnot P Q\\			
			\land PQ &\equiv \lnot \lor \lnot P \lnot Q\\
			\iff PQ &\equiv \land\implies PQ \implies QP\\
			\exists x P &\equiv \lnot \forall x \lnot P
		\end{align*}

		We will, now, provide \textit{syntax rules} for $\textbf{PForm}$ that prescribe the allowed ways we can combine elements from the alphabet to form meaningful strings.
		We begin with terms.		
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Terms]
			Let $U=\Sigma^*$ and let $B = \textbf{Var} \cup \textbf{Const}$.
			Now define a $U$ family of functions $\bv{g} = (g_n^{(\nu)})_{(\nu, n) \in \N^+ \times \N}$ such that $g_n^{(\nu)}: (\Sigma^*)^{\nu} \to \Sigma^*$ is defined by
			\begin{align*}
				g_n^{(\nu)}(\bv{\sigma}) = f_n^{(\nu)}\bv{\sigma}
			\end{align*}
			for $\bv{\sigma} \in (\Sigma^*)^\nu$.
			
			For example, suppose $\bv{\sigma} = (\bv{s}_0, \bv{s}_1, \bv{s}_2)$ 
			\begin{equation*}
				g_4^{(3)}(\bv{s}_0, \bv{s}_1, \bv{s}_2) = f_4^{(3)}\bv{s}_0\bv{s}_1\bv{s}_2
			\end{equation*}
			
			We then define $\textbf{Term}$ to be the $(U, B, \bv{g})$ inductive closure
			\begin{equation*}
				\textbf{Term} = N_{U, B, \bv{g}}
			\end{equation*}			
		\end{preformal definition}
	
		The definition of $t\in \textbf{Term}$ means that $t$ must take one or more of the following forms:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item{$t=v_n$ for some $n\in \N$ with $v_n \in \textbf{Var}$}
			\item{$t=c_n$ for some $n\in \N$ with $c_n \in \textbf{Const}$}
			\item{$t=f_n^{(\nu)}\bv{T}$ for some $\nu \in \N^+$, $n\in \N$ with $f_n^{(\nu)} \in \textbf{Func}$ and $\bv{T} \in \textbf{Term}^\nu$.}
		\end{itemize}
		
		We want to prove that $\textbf{Term}$ exhibits unique readability which will mean that $t\in \textbf{Term}$ must take exactly one of the forms above.
		Proving unique readability of $\textbf{Term}$ will allow us to define recursive function on $\textbf{Term}$ which we will require for our syntactic analysis.
		To do this we require a couple technical results about the string structure of terms.
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[List Suffix]
			If $l$ is a list then for any $1\le i \le \norm{l}$, the list $l_{i:\norm{l}}$ is a suffix for $l$
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[The Suffix of a Term is Always Equal to the concatenation of a List of Terms]			
			If $t \in \textbf{Term}$ then if $t'$ is a suffix of $t$ then there is some $\nu\in \N^+$ and $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^\nu$ such that $t' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t})$.
			
			We prove this by induction on the string length.
			We prove that for $\ell \in \N^+$, if $t\in \textbf{Term}$ and $\norm{t} = \ell$ then if $t'$ is a suffix of $t$ then there exists $\nu\in \N^+$ and $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu}$ such that $t' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t})$.
			
			If $\ell=1$ then $t$ is the only suffix of $t$ and it is already a term so we take $\bv{t} = (t) \in \textbf{Term}^1$ and $t = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}) = \text{Cat}(t)$.
			
			Now suppose the hypothesis holds for all $\ell' < \ell$ and consider
			\begin{equation*}
				t = f_n^{(\nu)} \bv{t} = \text{Cat}(f_n^{(\nu)}, \bv{t})
			\end{equation*}
			with $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu}$.
			
			Consider a suffix $t' = t_{i:\norm{t}}$.
			If $i=1$ then $t' = t = \text{Cat}(t)$ as required.
			Now suppose $i > 1$.
			Then there is some $1 \le j \le \nu$ such that
			\begin{equation*}
				t' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t})_{i-1:\ell} = \text{Cat}((t_j)_{i':\norm{t_j}}, \bv{t}_{j+1:\nu})
			\end{equation*}
			where $i-1 = \norm{\text{Cat}(\bv{t}_{1:j-1})} + i'$ so $1 \le i' = i - 1 - \norm{\text{Cat}(\bv{t}_{1:j-1})} \le \norm{t_j}$.
			We note that tuple slice $(t_j)_{i':\norm{t_j}} \in \Sigma^*$ and tuple slice $\bv{t}_{j+1:\nu}\in (\Sigma^*)^*$.
			In other words, slicing $\bv{t}$ leaves us with a suffix, within $(\Sigma^*)^*$ of the form $\bv{t}_{j+1:\nu}$ succeeding $(t_j)_{i':\norm{t_j}}$, a suffix of the term $t_j$.
			Terms $\bv{t}_{1:j-1}$ have been removed by the slice.
			
			But consider $(t_j)_{i':\norm{t_j}}$. 
			This is the suffix of a term, so this means that it is equal to $\text{Cat}(\bv{t}')$ for some $\bv{t'} \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu'}$ for some $\nu' \in \N^+$.
			But, this means that
			\begin{equation*}
				t' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}', \bv{t}_{j+1:\nu}) = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}'')
			\end{equation*}
			with $\bv{t}'' = \bv{t}' \circ \bv{t}_{j+1:\nu}$, recalling that $\circ: (\Sigma^*)^* \times (\Sigma^*)^* \to (\Sigma^*)^*$ is binary concatenation on $(\Sigma^*)^*\supset \textbf{Term}^*$.
			
			This concludes our proof that if $t'$ is a suffix of $t$ then there is some $\nu\in N^+$ and $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu}$ such that $t' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t})$.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Equality of the Concatenation of a Tuple of Terms Implies Equality of the Tuples]
			Suppose $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu}$ and $\bv{t}' \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu'}$ and suppose
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Cat}(\bv{t}) = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}').
			\end{equation*}
			We will prove that under these conditions $\nu'=\nu$ and $\bv{t}' = \bv{t}$.
			
			We proceed by (strong) induction on the length of the string.
			Our induction statement is the following.
			Consider $\ell\in \N^+$ and suppose $\norm{\text{Cat}(\bv{t})} = \ell$ with $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu}$. 
			Suppose also that $\bv{t}' \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu'}$ and $\text{Cat}(\bv{t}') = \text{Cat}(\bv{t})$.
			Then $\nu' = \nu$ and $\bv{t}' = \bv{t}$.
			
			If $\norm{\text{Cat}(\bv{t})} = 1$ then $\bv{t}$ must contain a single term $t$, and this term must be 0-ary so that $\bv{t} = (f_n^{(0)})$ for some $n$.
			But if $\text{Cat}(\bv{t}') = f_n^{(0)}$ then $\bv{t}' = (f_n^{(0)}) = \bv{t}$.
			
			Now suppose $\norm{\text{Cat}(\bv{t})} = \ell$ and the induction hypothesis holds for any $\bv{s}$ with $\norm{\text{Cat}(\bv{s})} < \ell$.
			Consider
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Cat}(\bv{t}) = \text{Cat}(t_1, \bv{t}_{2:\nu}) = \text{Cat}(t'_1, \bv{t}'_{2:\nu'}) = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}').
			\end{equation*}
			Now consider $t_1$ and $t'_1$.
			Suppose, without loss of generality, that $\norm{t'_1} \ge \norm{t_1}$. 
			Then
			\begin{equation*}
				t'_1 = \text{Cat}(t'_1) = \text{Cat}(t_1, (t'_1)_{\norm{t_1}+1:\norm{t'_1}}) = \text{Cat}(t_1, \bv{t}'')
			\end{equation*}
			noting that $(t'_1)_{\norm{t_1}+1:\norm{t'_1}} = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}'')$ with $\bv{t}'' \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu''}$ for some $\nu'' \in \N^+$.
			This follows because $(t'_1)_{\norm{t_1}+1:\norm{t'_1}}$ is a suffix of $t'_1$ and applyting the previous theorem.
			But, $\norm{t'_1} < \ell$, so this means the induction hypothesis applies so since $\text{Cat}(t'_1) = \text{Cat}(t_1, \bv{t}'')$ we must have $(t'_1) = (t_1) \circ \bv{t}''$ but $(t'_1)$ only has length 1 within $(\Sigma^*)^*$ so $\bv{t}''$ must have length 0 and we must have $(t'_1) = (t_1)$ which means $t'_1 = t_1$.
			But, it then follows that
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Cat}(\bv{t}_{2:\nu}) = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}'_{2:\nu'})
			\end{equation*}
			from which we conclude, again, by the induction hypothesis, that $\bv{t}_{2:\nu} = \bv{t}'_{2:\nu'}$ so that $\nu' = \nu$ and since $t'_1 = t_1$, we have overall that $\bv{t}' = \bv{t}$ as required.			
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[$\textbf{Term}$ Exhibits Unique Readability]
			We now prove that $\textbf{Term}$ exhibits unique readability by demonstrating that is is freely inductively generated.
			
			For each $g_n^{\nu}$ the first symbol in the output is $f_n^{(\nu)}$.
			This means the range of any two $g_n^{\nu}$ and $g_{n'}^{(\nu')}$ are disjoint because their outputs start with unequal function symbols.
			The ranges are also disjoint from $B = \textbf{Var} \cup \textbf{Const}$ because no $b\in B$ begins with a function symbol.
			
			Now we must show each $g_n^{(\nu)}$ is injective.
			Suppose
			\begin{equation*}
				g_n^{(\nu)}(\bv{t}) = f_n^{(\nu)}\bv{t} = f_n^{(\nu')} \bv{t}'= g_n^{(\nu')}(\bv{t}')
			\end{equation*}
			for $\bv{t}, \bv{t}' \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu}$.
			But, this means $\text{Cat}(\bv{t}') = \text{Cat}(\bv{t})$ which implies, by the previous theorem, that $\bv{t} = \bv{t}'$ which proves that $g_n^{(\nu)}$ is injective.
		\end{preformal theorem}
	
		\begin{preformal definition}[Atomic Formula]
			We define
			\begin{equation*}
				\textbf{Atom} = \{ P_n^{(\nu)} \bv{t} \in \Sigma^* \mid \bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu} \}
			\end{equation*}
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Permissive Formulas]
			Let $U = \Sigma^*$ and let $B = \textbf{Atom}$.
			We define $f_{\lnot}:\Sigma^* \to \Sigma^*$ and $f_{\implies}:(\Sigma^*)^2 \to \Sigma^*$
			\begin{align*}
				f_{\lnot}(\bv{\sigma}) =& \lnot \bv{\sigma}\\
				f_{\implies}(\bv{\sigma}, \bv{\tau}) =& \implies \bv{\sigma} \bv{\tau}\\
			\end{align*}
			and for each $x \in \textbf{Var}$ we define $f_x:\Sigma^* \to \Sigma^*$
			\begin{align*}
				f_x(\bv{\sigma}) =& \forall x \bv{\sigma}
			\end{align*}
			
			
			If we let $I = \textbf{Log}\cup \textbf{Var}$, we can see that $I$ can be used as an index set to form $\bv{f}$ a $U$ function family.
			
			We define
			\begin{equation*}
				\textbf{PForm} = N_{U, B, \bv{f}}
			\end{equation*}
			the set of permissive formulas.
			
			Permissive formulas are of the form 
			\begin{itemize}
				\item{$A$ where $A \in \textbf{Atom}$}
				\item{$\lnot P$ where $P \in \textbf{PForm}$}
				\item{$\implies PQ$ where $P, Q \in \textbf{PForm}$}
				\item{$\forall x P$ where $x\in \textbf{Var}$ and $P\in \textbf{PForm}$.}
			\end{itemize}
			
			We can see again that $\bv{f}$ satisfies the condition so that $\textbf{PForm}$ is freely inductively generated so that it exhibits unique readability.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		We want to prove that $\textbf{PForm}$ exhibits unique readabilty.
		To do so we will follow a similar strategy as we did to prove $\textbf{Term}$ exhibits unique readability.
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Every Suffix of a formula is a list of terms followed by a list of formulas]
			Suppose $P \in \textbf{PForm}$ and consider $P'$ is a suffix of $P$.
			We will prove that there exists $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^n$ and $\bv{P} \in \textbf{PForm}^m$ with $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
			\begin{equation*}
				P' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}, \bv{P})
			\end{equation*}
			Note that $\bv{t}$ may be empty.
			
			We prove this by induction on the string length $\ell$.
			We prove that for all $\ell \in \N^+$ that if $P \in \textbf{PForm}$ and $P'$ is a suffix of $P$ then there exists $\bv{t}\in \textbf{Term}^n$ and $\bv{P} \in \textbf{PForm}^m$ with $n, m \in \N$ such that $P' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}, \bv{P})$.
			
			Now suppose $\ell = 1$.
			The only permissive formulas of length 1 are of the form $P_n^{(0)}$, the 0-ary predicates.
			The only suffix of such a formula is itself and this is a length-1 sequence of permissive formulas.
			
			Now suppose $\norm{P} = \ell > 1$.
			
			Now suppose $P$ is atomic so that
			\begin{equation*}
				P = P_n^{(\nu)}\bv{t}
			\end{equation*}
			with $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^{\nu}$.
			Then a proper suffix of $P$ is of the form
			\begin{equation*}
				P' = \text{Cat}((t_j)_{i:\norm{t_j}}, \bv{t}_{j+1;\nu}) = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}', \bv{t}_{j+1:\nu}) = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}'')
			\end{equation*}
			for some $1 \le i \le \norm{t_j}$.
			This follows because $(t_j)_{i:\norm{t_j}}$ is a suffix of $t_j$ and $\bv{t}'' = \bv{t}'\circ \bv{t}_{j+1:\nu}$.
			
			Now suppose $P = \lnot Q$ with $Q \in \textbf{PForm}$.
			If $P' = P_{2:\norm{P}} = Q$ then we see $P' \in \textbf{PForm}$.
			If not then $P' = P_{i:\norm{P}} = Q_{i-1:\norm{Q}} = Q'$.
			But since $Q'$ is a suffix of a formula $Q$ with length $\norm{Q} = \norm{P}-1 = \ell - 1$ the induction hypothesis applies so that $P' = Q' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}, \bv{P})$ for some $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^n$ and $\bv{P} \in \textbf{PForm}^m$ for some $n, m \in \N$
			
			Now suppose $P = \implies QR$ with $Q, R \in \textbf{PForm}$.
			If $P' = P_{i:\norm{P}}$ and $i>1 + \norm{Q}$ then $P'$ is a suffix of $R'$ so the induction hypothesis applies.
			Otherwise, if $2\le i \le \norm{Q}+1$ then $P' = Q'R$ where $Q'$ is a suffix of $Q$ so
			\begin{equation*}
				P' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}, \bv{P}, R) = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}, \bv{P}')
			\end{equation*}
			with $\bv{P}' = \bv{P} \circ (R)$.
			
			Finally, suppose $P = \forall x Q$ with $x\in \textbf{Var}$.
			If $P' = 2:\norm{P} = x Q$ then we see that $P' = \text{Cat}(\bv{t}, \bv{P})$ with $\bv{t} = (x)$ and $\bv{P} = (Q)$.
			Otherwise $P'$ is a suffix of $Q'$ so the induction hypothesis applies.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Equality of the concatenation of a tuple of formulas implies equality of the formulas]
			We prove that for each $\ell \in \N^+$ that for $\bv{z} \in (\textbf{Term} \cup \textbf{PForm})^* = Z^*$ that if $\bv{z}' \in Z^*$ and $\text{Cat}(\bv{z}) = \text{Cat}(\bv{z}')$ then $\bv{z}' = \bv{z}$.
			
			If $\ell = 1$ then $\bv{z} = (z)$ with $z \in \textbf{Var} \cup \textbf{Const}$ or $z$ is a 0-ary predicate.
			In either case, we must have $\bv{z}' = (z)$ also so that $\text{Cat}(\bv{z}') = \text{Cat}(\bv{z})$.
			
			Now suppose $\bv{z} \in Z^{\nu}$ and $\bv{z}' \in Z^{\nu'}$ and consider
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Cat}(\bv{z}) = \text{Cat}(z_1, \bv{z}_{2:\nu}) = \text{Cat}(z'_1, \bv{z}_{2:\nu'}) = \text{Cat}(\bv{z}').
			\end{equation*}
			Suppose, without loss of generality, that $\norm{z'_1} \ge \norm{z_1}$ so that
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Cat}(z'_1) = \text{Cat}(z_1, x) = \text{Cat}(z_1, \bv{t}, \bv{P}) = \text{Cat}(z_1, \bv{z}'')
			\end{equation*}
			with $\bv{t} \in \textbf{Term}^*$ and $\bv{P} \in \textbf{PForm}^*$ and $\bv{z}'' = \bv{t} \circ \bv{P} \in Z^*$.
			This follows from previous theorems because $x$ is either the suffix of a term (in which case $\bv{P}$ is empty) or $x$ is the suffix of a permissive formula.
			But $\norm{z'_1} < \ell$ so the induction hypothesis applies. But this means $(z'_1) = (z_1) \circ \bv{z}$.
			But $(z'_1)$ and $(z_1)$ have length 1 in $Z^*$ so $\bv{z}$ must be empty from which we conclude $z'_1 = z_1$.
			
			But this means that
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Cat}(\bv{z}_{2:\nu}) = \text{Cat}(\bv{z}'_{2:\nu'})
			\end{equation*}
			but since $\norm{\text{Cat}(\bv{z}_{2:\nu})} < \ell$ the induction hypothesis applies.
			so we can conclude $\bv{z}'_{2:\nu'} = \bv{z}_{2:\nu}$ and since $z'_1 = z_1$, we can conclude $\bv{z}' = \bv{z}$.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Unique Readability of $\textbf{PForm}$]
			Suppose $P\in \textbf{PForm}$.
			
			Suppose $P \in \textbf{Atom}$. Then $P$ begins with a predicate symbol which means it is not a disjunction (doesn't begin with $\lnot$), implication (doesn't begin with $\implies$), or quantification (doesn't begin with $\forall$).
			
			Suppose $P$ begins with $\lnot$ so that $P = \lnot Q = \lnot Q'$. 
			But we can see that $Q=Q'$ so that $f_{\lnot}$ is injective.
			A similar argument follows if $P = \forall x Q$.
			
			If $P = \implies QR$ and suppose also that $P = \implies Q'R'$.
			But, the previous theorem applies to $(Q, R)$ tells us that since $QR = Q'R'$ that $(Q, R) = (Q', R')$ so thta $f_{\implies}$ is injective.
			
			Now suppose $P$ begins with $\forall x$ so that $P = \forall x Q$ and suppose also $P = \forall x Q'$.
			Again, similar to the $\lnot$ case, we can see $Q = Q'$ so that $f_x$ is injective.
			
			So we can see that $\textbf{PForm}$ is freely inductively generated and therefore exhibits unique readability.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		It is a great benefit that $\textbf{PForm}$ exhibits unique readability, because this means we can now make recursive function definitions on $\textbf{PForm}$ which we will proceed to do below.

		We use Polish notation throughout, i.e. $\implies PQ$ instead of $P \implies Q$ or $fx_1 x_2 x_3$ instead of $f(x_1, x_2, x_3)$.
		For human readability, we will often \textit{abbreviate} the polish notation into more standard notation including parentheses.
		Polish notation will be useful for the metalogical proof whereas the parentheses notation is more useful for human readability and understanding.
	
		There are two problems with quantification within $\textbf{PForm}$.
		\begin{itemize}
			\item{Strings of the form $\forall x P$ are allowed in $\textbf{PForm}$ even though the variable $x$ doesn't appear in $P$. This is called redundant quantification.}
			\item{Strings of the form $\forall x \forall x Px$ are allowed in $\textbf{PForm}$. The same variable $x$ is captured in multiple nested quantification scopes. This is called repeated quantification.}
		\end{itemize}
		We will define a new set, the strict formulas called $\textbf{SForm}$ or just $\textbf{Form}$ which forbid these two degenerate behaviors.
		However, to do this, we need to be able to detect which variables are free and bound within a permissive formula.
		
		Because $\textbf{Term}$ and $\textbf{PForm}$ exhibit unique readability, we can use the recursion theorem to recursively define function on these sets.
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Free Variables]
			We define a function $\text{Free}: \textbf{PForm} \cup \textbf{Term} \to \mc{P}(\textbf{Var})$ which maps a formula $P$ in $\textbf{PForm}$ to the set of variables which appear in $P$ but which are not within the scope of a quantifier.
			
			First we define $\text{Free}_T: \textbf{Term} \to \mc{P}(\textbf{Var})$
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Free}_T(t) = \begin{cases}
					\emptyset &\text{ if } t \in \textbf{Const}\\					
					\{t\} &\text{ if } t\in \textbf{Var}\\
					\bigcup_{i\in [\nu]} \left(\text{Free}_T^{(\nu)}(\bv{t})\right)_i &\text{ if } t=f_n^{(\nu)}\bv{t}
				\end{cases}
			\end{equation*}
			and then we define $\text{Free}_P: \textbf{PForm} \to \mc{P}(\textbf{Var})$
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Free}(P) = \begin{cases}
					\bigcup_{i\in [\nu]} \left(\text{Free}_T^{(\nu)}(\bv{t})\right)_i &\text{ if } P = P_n^{(\nu)}\bv{t}\\
					\text{Free}(Q) &\text{ if } P = \lnot Q\\
					\text{Free}(Q) \cup \text{Free}(R) &\text{ if } P = \implies QR\\
					\text{Free}(Q) \setminus \{x\} &\text{ if } P = \forall x Q
				\end{cases}
			\end{equation*}
			then we define $\text{Free}: \textbf{PForm} \cup \textbf{Term} \to \mc{P}(\textbf{Var})$ by
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Free}(X) = \begin{cases}
					\text{Free}_T(X) &\text{ if } X \in \textbf{Term}\\
					\text{Free}_P(X) &\text{ if } X \in \textbf{PForm}.
				\end{cases}
			\end{equation*}
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Bound Variables]
			We define another function $\text{Bnd}: \textbf{PForm} \to \mc{P}(\textbf{Var})$ by
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Bnd}(P) = \begin{cases}
					\emptyset &\text{ if } P \in \textbf{Atom}\\
					\text{Bnd}(Q) &\text{ if } P = \lnot Q\\
					\text{Bnd}(Q) \cup \text{Bnd}(R) &\text{ if } P = \implies QR\\
					\text{Bnd}(Q) \cup \{x\} &\text{ if } P = \forall x Q
				\end{cases}
			\end{equation*}
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[All Variables in a Formula]
			We define $\text{Vars}: \textbf{PForm} \cup \textbf{Term} \to \mc{P}(\textbf{Var})$ by
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Vars}(X) = \begin{cases}
					\text{Free}(X) &\text{ if } X \in \textbf{Term}\\
					\text{Free}(X) \cup \text{Bnd}(X) &\text{ if } X \in \textbf{PForm}
				\end{cases}
			\end{equation*}
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Open and Closed Formulas and Terms]
			Suppose $X \in \textbf{PForm} \times \textbf{Term}$. 
			If $\text{Free}(X) = \emptyset$ we say $X$ is a closed term or formula, otherwise we say $X$ is an open term or formula.
			
			A closed term still have variables which need to be \qq{plugged in} before that term represents a single specific object.
			If a formula is open then this means it still has variables that need to be plugged in before it can be considered to be a \qq{complete statement}.
			From a semantic perspective, a closed formula is one for which we can assign a truth value, whereas an open formula requires that we first plug in closed terms for all variables.
			
			We define
			\begin{align*}
				\textbf{CTerm} =& \{t \in \textbf{Term} \mid \text{Free}(t) = \emptyset\}\\
				\textbf{CPForm} =& \{P \in \textbf{PForm} \mid \text{Free}(P) = \emptyset,
			\end{align*}
			the sets fo closed terms and permissive formulas.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Strict Formulas]
			Let $U = \textbf{PForm}$ and $B=\textbf{Atom} \subset \textbf{PForm}$.
			We again define $f_{\lnot}: \textbf{PForm} \to \textbf{PForm}$ and $f_{\implies}: \textbf{PForm}^2 \to \textbf{PForm}$ by
			\begin{align*}
				f_{\lnot}(P) =& \lnot P\\
				f_{\implies}(P, Q) =& \implies PQ.
			\end{align*}
			For each $x\in \textbf{Var}$ we then define
			\begin{equation*}
				D_x = \{P \in \textbf{PForm} \mid x\in \text{Free}(P) \text{ and } x \not \in \text{Bnd}(P)\}
			\end{equation*}
			and $f_x: D_x \subset \textbf{PForm} \to \textbf{PForm}$ by
			\begin{equation*}
				f_x(P) = \forall x P.
			\end{equation*}
			We then collect $f_{\lnot}, f_{\implies}, f_x$ into $\bv{f}$ a $U$-function family and let
			\begin{equation*}
				\textbf{SForm} = N_{U, B, \bv{f}}
			\end{equation*}
			
			$\textbf{SForm}$ is the subset of $\textbf{PForm}$ which excludes redundant and repeated quantification.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Finite Set]
			Suppose $S$ is a set.
			If there exists an $n\in \N$ and a bijection $f:S \to [n]$ then we say $S$ is finite.
			
			We define
			\begin{equation*}
				\mc{P}_{\text{fin}}(S) = \{s \in \mc{P}(S) \mid s \text{ is finite}\}.
			\end{equation*}
			
			If $M \subset \N$ is finite then there exists a unique $m\in M$ such that $n < m$ for all $n\in M$ with $n\neq m$.
			We call this element $\text{max}(M)$.
			
			We note, but do not prove, that $\text{Free}(P)$ and $\text{Bnd}(P)$ are finite for each $P \in \textbf{PForm}$.
			It could be proven by induction.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Fresh Variable]
			We define a function $\text{Fresh}: \mc{P}_{\text{Fin}}(\textbf{Var}) \to \textbf{Var}$ by
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Fresh}(V) = \begin{cases} v_0 &\text{ if } V = \emptyset\\

			\begin{align*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\lnot I, (j, k), \curlywedge)}) = \{\bv{l} \in L^* \mid C_k(\bv{l}) = \curlywedge\}
			\end{align*}
			and the new line is
			\begin{align*}
				(n, \lnot C_j(\bv{l}), D_k(\bv{l}) \setminus \{j\}, \lnot I, (j, k), \curlywedge)
			\end{align*}
			
			Rule $\lnot E$
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\lnot E, (j, k), \curlywedge)}) = \{\bv{l}\in L^* \mid C_k(\bv{l}) = \lnot C_j(\bv{l})\}
			\end{align*}
			and the new line is
			\begin{align*}
				(n, \curlywedge, D_j(\bv{l}) \cup D_k(\bv{l}), \lnot E,  (j, k), \curlywedge)
			\end{align*}
			
			Rule $\text{DN}$
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\text{DN}, (j), \curlywedge)}) = \{\bv{l} \in L^* \mid C_j(\bv{l}) \in \textbf{PForm}_{\text{DN}}\}
			\end{equation*}
			and the new line is
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, \text{Body}_{\text{DN}}(C_j(\bv{l})), D_j(\bv{l}), \text{DN}, (j), \curlywedge)
			\end{equation*}
			
			Rule $\forall I$
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\forall I, (j), (t, v))}) = \{&\bv{l}\in L^* \mid t\in \text{Terms}(C_j(\bv{l}))\\ 
				&\text{and } t\not \in \text{Terms}(C_d(\bv{l})) \text{ for each } d \in D_j(\bv{l})\}
			\end{align*}
			and the new line is
			\begin{align*}
				(n, \forall v \text{GenAll}_{t, v}(C_j(\bv{l})), D_j(\bv{l}), \forall I, (j), (t, v))
			\end{align*}
			
			Rule $\forall E$
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\forall E, (j), t)}) = \{\bv{l} \in L^* \mid C_j(\bv{l}) \in \textbf{PForm}_{\forall}\}
			\end{align*}
			and the new line is
			\begin{align*}
				(n, \text{Rep}_{\text{QVar}(C_j(\bv{l})), t}(\text{body}_{\forall}(C_j(\bv{l}))), D_j(\bv{l}), \forall E, (j), t)
			\end{align*}
		\end{preformal definition}
	
		\begin{preformal definition}[Valid Proofs]
			Let $U = \textbf{Proof}$, let $B = \{()\} \subset U=\textbf{Proof}$ and let $\bv{R}$ be a valid deduction rule family such as the rules of natural deduction.
			We then define
			\begin{equation*}
				\textbf{VProof} = N_{\textbf{Proof}, \{()\}, \bv{R}}.
			\end{equation*}
			A valid proof is an element of the inductive closure under the deduction rules.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Derivability Judgement]
			Consider $\Gamma \subset \textbf{CForm}$ and $C \in \textbf{CForm}$.
			We say $C$ is derivable (under deduction rule family $\bv{R}$ from $\Gamma$ if there exists $\bv{p} \in \textbf{Proof}$ and there is some $n$ such that 
			\begin{align*}
				C_n(\bv{p}) =& C\\
				\Gamma =& \{C_m(\bv{p}) \in \textbf{CForm} \mid m\in D_n(\bv{p})\}
			\end{align*}
			Under this condition we say
			\begin{equation*}
				\Gamma \vdash_{\bv{R}} C
			\end{equation*}
			or simply $\Gamma \vdash C$.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Theory]
			If $\Gamma \subset \textbf{CForm}$ then
			\begin{equation*}
				\textbf{Th}(\Gamma, \bv{R}) = \{C \in \textbf{CForm} \mid \Gamma_{\bv{R}} \vdash C\}
			\end{equation*}
			The set $\Gamma$ is called the axioms of the theory.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[Extension By Rule Addition]
			Suppose $\bv{R} = (R_{(r, \bv{a}, p)})_{(r, \bv{a}, p) \in I}$ is a valid deduction rule family.
			Now suppose $I'$ is another index set and $I \subset I'$ and $\bv{R}'$ is a valid deduction rule family under index set $I'$.
			We say that $\bv{R}'$ is an extension of $\bv{R}$.
			We say that $\textbf{Th}(\Gamma, \bv{R}')$ is the extension of $\textbf{Th}(\Gamma, \bv{R})$ by rule addition.
			
			It can be seen that $\textbf{Th}(\Gamma, \bv{R}) \subset \textbf{Th}(\Gamma, \bv{R}')$.
			If it is the case that $\textbf{Th}(\Gamma, \bv{R}') \subset \textbf{Th}(\Gamma, \bv{R})$ then we say the extension is conservative.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}
			We introduce our first derived rule call duplication and denoted by $\text{DUP}$.
			This rule has $n_{\text{DUP}}^{(R)} = 1$, $P_{\text{POE}}^{(R)} = \{\curlywedge\}$ and domain
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\text{DUP}, (j), \curlywedge)}) = L^*
			\end{equation*}
			with new line
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, C_j(\bv{l}), D_j(\bv{l}), \text{DUP}, j, \curlywedge)
			\end{equation*}
			This rule simply duplicates a previous line.
			We will primarily use it when we want to show an alias for a formula on one line, but show the actual formula on another.
			For example, $\implies \lnot P Q$ is aliased by $\lor PQ$.
			We might start a proof with $\lor PQ$ but then want to show $\implies \lnot P Q$ is the actual (not aliased) formula.
			We may want to use it to simply move an expression from an earlier line down to the end of the proof just as a visual aid.
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[Principle of Explosion]
			The following meta proof establishes that if we have derived $\curlywedge$ on any line of a proof with dependencies $D$, then we may derive any formula $P$ on a later line, also with dependencies $D$.
			\begin{equation*}
				\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
					\text{line} & \text{formula} & \text{dependencies} & \text{rule} & \text{citations} & \text{parameters}\\ \hline
					n_1 & \curlywedge & D_{n_1} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_2 & \lnot P & \{n_2\} & \text{Assume} & () & \lnot P\\
					n_3 & \lnot \lnot P & D_{n_1} & \lnot I & (n_1, n_2) & \curlywedge\\
					n_4 & P & D_{n_1} & \text{DN} & (n_3) & \curlywedge
				\end{array}
			\end{equation*}		
			We can codify this into a rule with index name $\text{POE}$, $n_{\text{POE}}^{(R)} = 1$, and $P_{\text{POE}}^{(R)} = \textbf{CForm}$.
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\text{POE}, (j), C)}) = \{\bv{l} \in L^* \mid C_j(\bv{l}) = \curlywedge\}
			\end{equation*}
			with new line
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, P, D_j(\bv{l}), \text{POE}, (j), P)
			\end{equation*}
			The metaproof above establishes that extension by $R_{\text{POE}}$ is conservative. because if $\Gamma \vdash_{\bv{R}'} C$, we can see that we can use the metaproof pattern above so that $\Gamma \vdash_{\bv{R}} C$. 
			This is proven formally using structural induction on $\textbf{VProof}'$, but the only non-trivial step is showing that application of the new rule results in a deduction judgement that is valid without that rule.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[$\lor E$]
			We let
			\begin{equation*}
				\lor PQ
			\end{equation*}
			be an alias for
			\begin{equation*}
				\lnot P \implies Q
			\end{equation*}
			We define 
			\begin{equation*}
				\textbf{PForm}_{\lor} = \{\lor PQ \in \textbf{PForm}\}
			\end{equation*}
			and define $\text{Body}_{\lor, L}, \text{Body}_{\lor, R}: \textbf{PForm}_{\lor} \to \textbf{PForm}$ by
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Body}_{\lor, L}(\lor PQ) =& P\\
				\text{Body}_{\lor, R}(\lor PQ) =& Q\\				
			\end{align*}
						
			We now establish a rule $\lor E$
			
			\begin{equation*}
				\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
					\text{line} & \text{formula} & \text{dependencies} & \text{rule} & \text{citations} & \text{parameters}\\ \hline
					n_1& \implies \lnot P Q& D_{n_1} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots \\
					n_2 & \implies PR & D_{n_2} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_3 & \implies QR & D_{n_3} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_4 & \lnot R & \{n_4\} & \text{Assume} & () & \lnot R\\
					n_5 & P & \{n_5\} & \text{Assume} & () & P\\
					n_6 & R & D_{n_2} \cup \{n_5\} & \implies E & (n_2, n_5) & \curlywedge\\
					n_7 & \curlywedge & D_{n_2} \cup \{n_4, n_5\} & \lnot E & (n_6, n_4) & \curlywedge\\
					n_8 & \lnot P & D_{n_2} \cup \{n_4\} & \lnot I & (n_5, n_7) & \curlywedge\\
					n_9 & Q & D_{n_1} \cup D_{n_2} \cup \{n_4\} & \implies E & (n_1, n_8) & \curlywedge\\
					n_{10} & R & D_{n_1} \cup D_{n_2} \cup D_{n_3} \cup \{n_4\} & \implies E & (n_3, n_9) & \curlywedge\\
					n_{11} & \curlywedge & D_{n_1} \cup D_{n_2} \cup D_{n_3} \cup \{n_4\} & \lnot E & (n_{10}, n_4) & \curlywedge\\
					n_{12} & \lnot \lnot R & D_{n_1} \cup D_{n_2} \cup D_{n_3} & \lnot I & (n_4, n_{11}) & \curlywedge\\
					n_{13} & R & D_{n_1} \cup D_{n_2} \cup D_{n_3} & \text{DN} & (12) & \curlywedge
				\end{array}
			\end{equation*}			
			
			and introduce the rule $\lor E$ with $n_{\lor E}^{(R)} = 3$, $P_{\lor E}^{(R)} = \{\curlywedge\}$:
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\lor E, (i, j, k), \curlywedge)}) = \{&\bv{l} \in L^* \mid \text{Ant}(C_j(\bv{l})) = \text{Body}_{\lor, L}(C_i(\bv{l}))\\ &\text{and } \text{Ant}(C_k(\bv{l})) = \text{Body}_{\lor, R}(C_i(\bv{l}))\\
				&\text{and } \text{Con}(C_j(\bv{l})) = \text{Con}(C_k(\bv{l}))\}
			\end{align*}
			with new line
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, \text{Con}(C_j(\bv{l})), D_i(\bv{l}) \cup D_j(\bv{l}) \cup D_k(\bv{l}), \lor E, (i, j, k), \curlywedge)
			\end{equation*}
			
			Extension by this rule is conservative, via this metaproof.
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[$\lor I$]
			If we have derived $P$ then we can derive $lor PQ$ or $\lor QP$ for any $Q$.
			We prove this now.
			
			\begin{equation*}
				\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
					\text{line} & \text{formula} & \text{dependencies} & \text{rule} & \text{citations} & \text{parameters}\\ \hline
					n_1 & P & D_{n_1} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_2 & \lnot P & \{n_2\} & \text{Assume} & () & \lnot P\\
					n_3 & \curlywedge & D_{n_1} \cup \{n_2\} & \lnot E & (n_1, n_2) & \curlywedge \\
					n_4 & Q & D_{n_1} \cup \{n_2\} & \text{POE} & (n_3) & Q\\
					n_5 & \implies \lnot P Q & D_{n_1} & \implies I & (n_1, n_4) & \curlywedge
				\end{array}
			\end{equation*}
			So we introduce the rule $\lor I_L$ with $n_{\lor I_L}^{(R)} = 1$ and $P_{\lor I_L}^{(R)} = \textbf{CForm}$ with
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\lor I_L, (j), C)}) = L^*
			\end{equation*}
			and new line
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, \lor C_j(\bv{l}) C, D_j(\bv{l}), \lor I_L, (j), C)
			\end{equation*}
			
			And also a rule for right $\lor I_R$:
			
			\begin{equation*}
				\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
					\text{line} & \text{formula} & \text{dependencies} & \text{rule} & \text{citations} & \text{parameters}\\ \hline
					n_1 & \implies \lnot P Q & D_{n_1} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_2 & \lnot Q & \{n_2\} & \text{Assume} & () & \lnot Q\\
					n_3 & \lnot P & \{n_3\} & \text{Assume} & () & P\\
					n_4 & Q & D_{n_1} \cup \{n_3\} & \implies E & (n_1, n_3) & \curlywedge\\
					n_5 & \curlywedge & D_{n_1} \cup \{n_2, n_3\} & \lnot E & (n_4, n_2) & \curlywedge\\
					n_6 & \lnot \lnot P & D_{n_1} \cup \{n_2\} & \lnot I & (n_3, n_5) & \curlywedge\\
					n_7 & P & D_{n_1} \cup \{n_2\} & \text{DN} & (n_6) & \curlywedge\\
					n_8 & \implies \lnot Q P & D_{n_1} & \implies I & (n_2, n_7) & \curlywedge
				\end{array}
			\end{equation*}			
			So we introduce the rule $\lor I_R$ with $n_{\lor I_R}^{(R)} = 1$ and $P_{\lor I_R}^{(R)} = \textbf{CForm}$ with
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\lor I_L, (j), C)}) = L^*
			\end{equation*}
			and new line
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, \lor C C_j(\bv{l}), D_j(\bv{l}), \lor I_L, (j), C)
			\end{equation*}
		\end{preformal theorem}
	
		\begin{preformal definition}[$\land E$]
			We let
			\begin{equation*}
				\land PQ
			\end{equation*}
			be an alias for
			\begin{equation*}
				\lnot \lor \lnot P \lnot Q
			\end{equation*}
			and define
			\begin{equation*}
				\textbf{PForm}_{\land} = \{\land PQ \in \textbf{PForm}\}
			\end{equation*}
			and $\text{Body}_{\land, L}, \text{Body}_{\land, R}: \textbf{PForm}_{\land} \to \textbf{PForm}$
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Body}_{\land, L}(\land PQ) =& P\\
				\text{Body}_{\land, R}(\land PQ) =& Q\\				
			\end{align*}
			
			and establish two $\land E$ rules
			
			\begin{equation*}
				\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
					\text{line} & \text{formula} & \text{dependencies} & \text{rule} & \text{citations} & \text{parameters}\\ \hline			
					n_1 & \lnot \lor \lnot P \lnot Q & D_{n_1} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_2 & \lnot P & \{n_2\} & \text{Assume} & () & \lnot P\\
					n_3 & \lor \lnot P \lnot Q & \{n_2\} & \lor I_L & (n_2) & \lnot Q\\
					n_4 & \curlywedge & D_{n_1} \cup \{n_2\} & \lnot E & (n_3, n_1) & \curlywedge\\
					n_5 & \lnot \lnot P & D_{n_1} & \lnot I & (n_2, n_4) & \curlywedge\\
					n_6 & P & D_{n_1} & \text{DN} & (n_5) & \curlywedge
				\end{array}
			\end{equation*}
			So we establish a new rule $\land E_L$ with $n_{\land E_L}^{(R)} = 1$ and $P_{\land E_L}^{(R)} = \{\curlywedge\}$
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\land E_L, (j), \curlywedge)}) = \{\bv{l} \in L^* \mid  C_j(\bv{l}) \in \textbf{PForm}_{\land}\}\\
			\end{equation*}
			with new line
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, \text{Body}_{\land, L}, D_j(\bv{l}), \land E_L, (j), \curlywedge)
			\end{equation*}
			
			\begin{equation*}
				\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
					\text{line} & \text{formula} & \text{dependencies} & \text{rule} & \text{citations} & \text{parameters}\\ \hline			
					n_1 & \lnot \lor \lnot P \lnot Q & D_{n_1} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_2 & \lnot Q & \{n_2\} & \text{Assume} & () & \lnot Q\\
					n_3 & \lor \lnot P \lnot Q & \{n_2\} & \lnot I_R & (n_2) & \lnot P\\ 
					n_4 & \curlywedge & D_{n_1} \cup \{n_2\} & \lnot E & (n_3, n_1) & \curlywedge\\
					n_5 & \lnot \lnot Q & D_{n_1} & \lnot I & (n_2, n_4) & \curlywedge\\
					n_6 & Q & D_{n_1} & \text{DN} & (n_5) & \curlywedge
				\end{array}
			\end{equation*}			
			
			So we establish a new rule $\land E_R$ with $n_{\land E_R}^{(R)} = 1$ and $P_{\land E_R}^{(R)} = \{\curlywedge\}$
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\land E_R, (j), \curlywedge)}) = \{\bv{l} \in L^* \mid  C_j(\bv{l}) \in \textbf{PForm}_{\land}\}\\
			\end{equation*}
			with new line
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, \text{Body}_{\land, R}, D_j(\bv{l}), \land E_R, (j), \curlywedge)
			\end{equation*}			
			
		\end{preformal definition}
		
		\begin{preformal definition}[$\land I$]
			\begin{equation*}
				\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
					\text{line} & \text{formula} & \text{dependencies} & \text{rule} & \text{citations} & \text{parameters}\\ \hline		
					n_1 & P & D_{n_1} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_2 & Q & D_{n_2} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_3 & \lor \lnot P \lnot Q & \{n_3\} & \text{Assume} & () & \lor \lnot P \lnot Q\\
					n_4 & \lnot P & \{n_4\} & \text{Assume} & () & \lnot P\\
					n_5 & \curlywedge & D_{n_1} \cup \{n_4\} & \lnot E & (n_1, n_4) & \curlywedge\\
					n_6 & \implies \lnot P \curlywedge &  D_{n_1} & \implies I & (n_4, n_5) & \curlywedge\\
					n_7 & \lnot Q & \{n_7\} & \text{Assume} & () & \lnot Q\\
					n_8 & \curlywedge & D_{n_2} \cup \{n_7\} & \lnot E & (n_2, n_7) & \curlywedge\\
					n_9 & \implies \lnot Q \curlywedge & D_{n_2} & \implies I & (n_7, n_8) & \curlywedge\\
					n_{10} & \curlywedge & D_{n_1} \cup D_{n_2} \cup \{n_3\} & \lor E & (n_3, n_6, n_9) & \curlywedge\\
					n_{11} & \lnot \lor \lnot P \lnot Q & D_{n_1} \cup D_{n_2} & \lnot I & (n_3, n_{10}) & \curlywedge
				\end{array}
			\end{equation*}				
			
			So we establish a new rule $\land I$ with $n_{\land I}^{(R)} = 2$ and $P_{\land I}^{(R)} = \{\curlywedge\}$ with
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\land I, (j, k), \curlywedge)}) = L^*
			\end{equation*}
			with new line
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, \land C_j(\bv{l})C_k(\bv{l}), D_j(\bv{l}) \cup D_k(\bv{l}), \land I, (j, k), \curlywedge)
			\end{equation*}
		\end{preformal definition}
			
		\begin{preformal theorem}[$\exists I$]
			We let
			\begin{equation*}
				\exists x P
			\end{equation*}
			be an alias for
			\begin{equation*}
				\lnot \forall x \lnot P
			\end{equation*}
			
			Suppose $t\in \textbf{CTerm}$, $v \not \in \text{Bnd}(P)$, $P' \in \text{Gen}_{t, v}(P)$ and $v\in \text{Free}(P')$.
			Then $\forall v P' \in \textbf{CForm}$.
			\begin{equation*}
				\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
					\text{line} & \text{formula} & \text{dependencies} & \text{rule} & \text{citations} & \text{parameters}\\ \hline
					n_1 & P & D_{n_1} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_2 & \forall v \lnot P' & \{n_2\} & \text{Assume} & () & \ \forall v \lnot P'\\
					n_3 & \lnot \text{Rep}_{v, t} P'& \{n_2\} & \forall E & (n_2) & \curlywedge\\
					n_4 & \lnot P & \{n_2\} & \text{DUP}& (n_3) & \curlywedge\\		
					n_5 & \curlywedge& D_{n_1} \cup \{n_2\} & \lnot E & (n_1, n_4) & \curlywedge\\
					n_6 & \lnot \forall v \lnot P' & D_{n_1} & \lnot E & (n_2, n_5) & \curlywedge\\
					n_7 & \exists v P' & D_{n_1} & \text{DUP} & (n_6) & \curlywedge				
				\end{array}
			\end{equation*}
			We had a few provisos on this proof. 
			First we needed $t\in \textbf{CTerm}$ and $v\not \in \text{Bnd}(P)$ so we could specify $P' \in \text{Gen}_{t, v}(P)$.
			But, we, futher, require that $v\in \text{Free}(P')$ so that we can quantify $\forall v P'$ and still get a strict (closed) formula.
			In the proof on line $n_3$ we used that since $P' \in \text{Gen}_{t, v}(P)$ that $\text{Rep}_{v, t}(P') = P$.
			
			With this proof we can introduce the rule $\exists I$ with $n_{\exists I}^{(R)} = 1$.
			We define
			\begin{equation*}
				D_{\exists I} = \{(P', t, v) \in \textbf{CForm} \times \textbf{Term} \times \textbf{Var} \mid v\not \in \text{Bnd}(P') \text{ and } v \in \text{Free}(P')\}
			\end{equation*}
			and let $P_{\exists I}^{(R)} = D_{\exists I}$.
			We then let
			\begin{equation*}
				\text{Dom}(R_{(\exists I, (j), (P', t, v))}) = \{\bv{l} \in L^* \mid P' \in \text{Gen}_{t, v}(C_j(\bv{l}))\}			
			\end{equation*}
			and the new line is
			\begin{equation*}
				(n, \exists v P', D_j, \exists I, (j), (P', t, v))
			\end{equation*}
		\end{preformal theorem}
		
		\begin{preformal theorem}[$\exists E$]
			Suppose $t \in \textbf{CTerm}$ and suppose that $t \not \in \text{Terms}(C_d(\bv{\ell}))$ for each $t\in \cup D_{n_2} \cup \{P, Q\}$.
			\begin{equation*}
				\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
					\text{line} & \text{formula} & \text{dependencies} & \text{rule} & \text{citations} & \text{parameters}\\ \hline
					n_1 & \exists v P & D_{n_1} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
					n_2 & \implies \text{Rep}_{v, t}(P) Q & D_{n_2} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots\\
